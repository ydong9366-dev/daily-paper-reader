# Federated Agentic AI for Interference-Aware Adaptive Spectrum Coordination 

Tomer Baziza Paz, Yaniv Cohen, and Kobi Cohen (Senior Member, IEEE) 

Abstract — Emerging intelligent wireless networks increasingly rely on autonomous AI agents to adapt spectrum usage to dynamic and heterogeneous operating conditions. However, col-lective learning is challenging when agents experience differ-ent local interference environments and cannot exchange raw observations due to communication-overhead and privacy con-straints. We propose Federated Agentic Spectrum Coordination with Interference Awareness (FASCIA), a federated multi-agent deep reinforcement learning framework for adaptive spectrum coordination. In FASCIA, each network manager acts as an autonomous AI agent that perceives its local interference environ-ment, accumulates experience in a persistent local memory, and learns an adaptive spectrum coordination policy through local interactions. Coordination among agents is achieved through periodic federated aggregation of locally trained models, without exchanging raw sensing data or experience. Since spectrum decisions made by one agent alter the environments observed by others, local learning is inherently heterogeneous and cou-pled. FASCIA employs proximal regularization to mitigate the resulting model drift and explicitly accounts for inter-carrier interference arising from partially overlapping channels. FASCIA is the first federated agentic AI framework to address this challenging interference-coupled spectrum coordination setting. Extensive simulations demonstrate that FASCIA achieves per-formance close to the centralized benchmark while avoiding raw-experience exchange and maintaining effectiveness across heterogeneous network configurations. 

Index Terms – Agentic AI, deep reinforcement learning (DRL), federated learning (FL), spectrum coordination, inter-carrier interference (ICI). 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The growing demand for wireless connectivity, coupled with spectrum scarcity and the increasing density of independently managed wireless networks, is driving a transition toward more autonomous and adaptive spectrum coordination. This trend is particularly pronounced in emerging 6G edge environments, where multiple networks may coexist within a shared spectrum band while experiencing rapidly changing traffic, topology, and interference conditions. Effective operation in such en-vironments requires more than static resource allocation or one-shot optimization. Network managers must continuously observe their local wireless environment, learn from previ-ous interactions, adapt their spectrum decisions to changing conditions, and coordinate their behavior with other networks. A. Challenges of Agentic Interference-Coupled Adaptive Spec-trum Coordination 

A fundamental challenge in ASC is that spectrum interac-tions are considerably more complex than those captured by conventional orthogonal-channel models. Many existing for-mulations assume perfectly orthogonal channels and therefore model interference primarily between networks selecting the same channel. In practical wireless systems, however, spectral leakage, imperfect filtering, and other hardware limitations give rise to inter-carrier interference (ICI), such that trans-missions over partially overlapping channels can significantly affect one another [6]. Consequently, the interference experi-enced by a network depends not only on co-channel transmis-sions but also on activity over neighboring channels, with the impact generally decreasing with spectral separation. Spectrum decisions therefore become interference-coupled: the effect of a local decision depends jointly on spectral separation, network topology, and the simultaneous decisions of other networks. This weighted, non-binary interference structure makes adaptive coordination substantially more challenging than conventional allocation under ideal channel orthogonality. The problem becomes even more challenging when spec-trum coordination must be learned autonomously and exe-cuted in a distributed manner. Each network manager (i.e., agent) has access primarily to its own sensing information and therefore possesses only a partial view of the global interference state, yielding a partially observable decision-making problem [3], [7], [8]. Moreover, the environment experienced by each network is inherently non-stationary: whenever one network modifies its spectrum usage, it changes the interference observed by other networks and consequently affects their observations, rewards, and subsequent decisions. Thus, the agents do not learn in isolated environments; rather, their local learning processes are intrinsically coupled through the shared wireless medium [4], [9]. At the same time, computing the optimal joint spectrum assignment generalizes graph coloring to weighted, non-binary interference and is computationally intractable [10], making repeated centralized optimization unsuitable for rapidly evolving network condi-tions. Finally, adaptation itself incurs a cost, since frequent arXiv:2610.39680v1 [cs.NI] 29 Oct 2026 2

channel changes interrupt ongoing communication and in-crease spectrum mobility. Hence, an effective ASC policy must react rapidly to changing interference conditions while minimizing unnecessary spectrum mobility. Beyond these decision-making challenges, enabling collec-tive learning among autonomous network managers introduces additional communication and privacy concerns. Centralized learning architectures may require locally collected sensing observations and interaction experiences to be transferred to a central entity, resulting in considerable communication overhead and exposing potentially sensitive operational infor-mation [11]–[13]. These limitations become increasingly im-portant as the number of autonomous networks grows and their local operating conditions become more heterogeneous [14], [15]. An effective learning architecture should therefore allow individual network managers to learn continuously from their local interactions while benefiting from knowledge acquired across the network, without requiring the exchange of raw sensing observations or interaction experiences. 

B. Federated Agentic AI under Interference Coupling 

Federated Agentic AI provides a natural framework for realizing this form of distributed intelligence. Rather than centralizing the experience collected throughout the network, autonomous AI agents can maintain and learn from their local observations and interaction histories while periodically sharing learned model information to support collective adap-tation [11], [12], [16]. This paradigm combines the closed-loop autonomy required for ASC with distributed knowledge sharing, allowing agents to adapt locally while coordinating their learning at the network level. Importantly, however, applying federated learning to interference-coupled wireless networks introduces challenges that differ from conventional federated settings: local environments are heterogeneous, and the experience acquired by each agent depends not only on its own spectrum decisions but also on the decisions of neighbor-ing agents through their shared interference environment [4], [9], [15]. Operating effectively under these conditions requires each agent to build its decisions from limited local information while retaining sufficient experience to learn the longer-term consequences of its actions. A network manager does not observe the underlying interference field directly. Instead, its users sense local channel conditions and report highly compressed indicators of whether their SINR satisfies the required QoS level. The manager aggregates these reports into a local quality representation that guides its spectrum decisions (Section II-C). This representation is intentionally compact, but necessarily provides only a partial view of the shared wireless environment. Moreover, the information avail-able to different managers is inherently heterogeneous, since their observations depend on their local topology, interference conditions, and served users [3], [7]. Learning from such observations requires experience to be accumulated over time. The consequence of a spectrum decision may extend beyond its immediate effect: a channel change modifies the interference experienced by neighboring networks, which may subsequently adapt their own decisions and thereby alter the environment observed by the original agent. A single interaction therefore provides only limited in-formation about the longer-term value of a spectrum decision. Rather than pooling these interactions in a centralized replay memory, the federated setting considered in this work allows each agent to maintain a persistent local memory of its own experience. This design keeps raw observations, actions, and rewards at their originating networks while allowing agents to continually refine their local decision policies [17], [18]. The resulting learning problem is particularly demanding because value estimation must remain effective under partial observability and interference-coupled local experience. To support stable local learning, we employ a target-network-free Mellowmax value learner (Section III-A). Its non-expansion backup operator provides a stable basis for extended phases of local model updates, which become especially important when communication with the federating entity is intentionally infrequent [19], [20]. Local adaptation alone, however, cannot provide effective spectrum coordination. The knowledge acquired independently by the agents must be combined so that their policies capture the interference and spatial-reuse relationships that extend beyond any single agent’s observations. In the proposed federated architecture, this coordination is achieved during training through periodic aggregation of locally learned model parameters, rather than through the exchange of raw sensing measurements or interaction experiences [15], [16]. At execu-tion time, each network manager acts autonomously using only its locally available information, without requiring message exchange with neighboring agents or a centralized controller. This architecture creates a fundamental tension between local learning and global coordination. Increasing the number of local updates between aggregation rounds reduces the frequency of model exchange and allows agents to exploit their persistent local experience, but it also increases the risk that their models drift apart as they learn from heterogeneous and interference-coupled local experience. Conversely, frequent aggregation can improve alignment among agents at the cost of greater communication [14], [15], [21]. The central challenge is therefore not merely to federate the learning process, but to preserve effective collective spectrum coordination when agents optimize over heterogeneous local experience that is coupled through interference. Addressing this challenge forms the basis for the FASCIA framework developed in this paper. 

C. Main Results 

Motivated by the challenges above, this work investigates how federated agentic AI can enable effective adaptive spec-trum coordination when autonomous network managers learn from heterogeneous local experience while remaining coupled through a shared interference environment. The objective is to retain the benefits of collective learning without requiring centralized collection of raw sensing observations or interac-tion histories, while accounting for the partial observability, interference coupling, and communication constraints inherent to distributed wireless operation. The main contributions and results of this work are summarized as follows. 3

1) Federated Agentic AI Framework for Interference-Coupled ASC: We formulate a challenging federated agentic AI setting for ASC, in which autonomous network managers learn and act based on partial local SINR observations while interacting through a shared interference environment. Unlike conventional federated learning settings, the agents do not interact with isolated local environments. Their operating con-ditions are heterogeneous, and the SINR observations available to each agent depend jointly on its own spectrum decisions and those of neighboring agents. Consequently, the local experiences used for learning are intrinsically coupled through the shared wireless medium. At the same time, collecting raw sensing observations and interaction histories at a central entity is undesirable due to communication overhead and privacy concerns. The resulting framework therefore requires agents to learn from heterogeneous and interference-coupled local experience, benefit from collective knowledge while limiting information exchange, and ultimately execute their spectrum decisions autonomously. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first federated agentic AI framework to address this chal-lenging interference-coupled spectrum coordination setting. 

2) Algorithm Development for Coupled and Heterogeneous Learning: To address this setting, we develop FASCIA (Federated Agentic Spectrum Coordination with Interference Awareness ), a federated multi-agent DRL algorithm tailored to the distinctive learning dynamics of interference-coupled ASC. Each agent continuously accumulates its locally acquired experience in a persistent replay buffer, retained across interac-tion episodes, and employs a target-network-free Mellowmax value learner to derive its spectrum-coordination policy under partial observability. At each federated round, agents perform 

E local learning updates using exclusively their own accumu-lated experience and periodically exchange only their learned model parameters for global aggregation; raw observations, actions, rewards, and transition tuples remain local. This design eliminates the need for centralized experience pooling while allowing agents to benefit from knowledge acquired across the network. The local learning problems, however, can differ substan-tially across network topologies, loads, user populations, and interference conditions. Moreover, the experience collected by different agents is not independent: the SINR measurements and rewards observed by one agent depend on the spec-trum decisions of neighboring agents through inter-network interference. Consequently, prolonged local optimization over heterogeneous and interference-coupled experience can drive the locally learned models in different directions, resulting in substantial model drift and degrading collective learning. To mitigate this effect, FASCIA incorporates proximal regular-ization into the federated optimization to penalize excessive deviation of locally updated models from the global model. We further investigate the effect of the number of local updates 

E on coordination performance under heterogeneous and interference-coupled local experience. The resulting algorithm supports collective learning during training while enabling each network manager to execute its learned spectrum policy autonomously and without inter-agent message exchange after training. 

3) Comprehensive Performance Evaluation: We conduct an extensive simulation study across heterogeneous network configurations, interference conditions, and deployment scales to evaluate both the spectrum-coordination performance and the learning behavior of FASCIA. The results demonstrate that FASCIA achieves performance close to the centralized benchmark despite relying on partial local observations and without centrally pooling raw interaction experience. We fur-ther compare alternative federated aggregation strategies and evaluate the effect of the number of local updates on learning behavior and coordination performance. The results show that FASCIA maintains strong performance across heterogeneous and interference-coupled operating conditions and generalizes effectively to network configurations beyond those encoun-tered during training. Overall, the experiments demonstrate that effective collective spectrum coordination can be achieved through federated agentic learning while keeping raw network experience local and retaining autonomous, message-free ex-ecution. 

4) Open-Source Implementation: To facilitate reproducibil-ity and further research, we make the implementation of FAS-CIA, together with the simulation and evaluation environment, publicly available as open-source code at [22]. The released code provides the training and evaluation pipeline required to reproduce the reported results and offers the research commu-nity a platform for further investigation of federated agentic AI, multi-agent learning, and adaptive spectrum coordination in interference-coupled wireless networks.

B. Federated Agentic Learning Model 

Spectrum coordination is carried out by a network manager associated with each network N n. In the proposed framework, the network manager acts as an autonomous AI agent: it ob-tains measurements generated within its own network, selects the operating channel, observes the resulting communication quality, and uses this interaction experience to improve its subsequent spectrum decisions. Thus, the agents operate lo-cally while remaining coupled through the shared wireless environment, since the channel selected by one network affects the interference experienced by the others. Unlike centralized-training approaches, we do not assume that the interaction experience generated by the different network managers is collected by a central learner. Instead, network manager n is identified with federated client n and retains locally the information generated through its inter-action with the spectrum environment. In particular, its per-user BSINR measurements, locally constructed observations, selected channels, received rewards, and stored interaction experience remain at the corresponding network. Coordination of the learning process is performed at the model level through periodic federated aggregation rather than by pooling the agents’ raw experience. This architecture is particularly relevant to the interference-coupled setting considered here. Although the agents ulti-mately learn a common spectrum coordination capability, the experience available to different network managers need not follow identical distributions. Networks may serve different numbers of users, experience different interference neighbor-hoods, and observe different channel conditions. Moreover, the actions taken by neighboring agents directly affect the local measurements observed by a given network. Consequently, the federated clients learn from heterogeneous and interference-coupled local experience. The above architecture specifies the information structure under which learning takes place: interaction experience re-mains local, while model information is periodically ex-changed for federated learning. The precise local learning ob-jective, model aggregation procedure, and the resulting FAS-CIA algorithm are developed in Section III. We emphasize that retaining raw experience locally provides an architectural separation between the participating networks; federation by itself, however, does not constitute a formal privacy guarantee. 6

D. Federated Training Procedure 

The local learning mechanisms described above are inte-grated into the federated training procedure summarized in Al-gorithm 2. At communication round t, the aggregation server broadcasts the current global model wt to the participating network managers. Each participating network manager then interacts with its local wireless environment as an autonomous AI agent, performs its spectrum decisions, and stores the resulting transitions in its persistent local replay buffer Dn.For a scenario containing Nt networks, the interaction phase consists of T · Nt steps, providing each agent with T decision points. Following the interaction phase, each client performs E

local gradient updates using mini-batches of size bz sampled 9

exclusively from its own replay buffer Dn. If the buffer contains fewer than bz transitions, the corresponding client skips the local update and returns the broadcast model un-changed. After completing the local updates, client n uploads its resulting model wt,E n to the aggregation server. The server then combines the uploaded models to obtain wt+1 , which initializes the next communication round. Thus, spectrum interaction and experience storage remain local to the network managers, whereas coordination across agents is performed through periodic model aggregation. Unlike conventional federated learning over fixed local datasets, the local experience used by FASCIA is generated through interaction with the wireless environment. Conse-quently, the contents of Dn evolve throughout training as the agent’s spectrum-coordination policy and the interference conditions it encounters change. Retaining the replay buffer across communication rounds allows local updates to draw from experience accumulated over multiple interactions rather than only from the most recently generated episode. Moreover, the network realization is regenerated across rounds, exposing the shared model to heterogeneous interference configurations during federated training rather than to a single fixed deploy-ment geometry. A. Simulation Setup and Performance Metrics

V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented FASCIA, a federated agentic AI framework for interference-aware adaptive spectrum coordination. In FASCIA, each network manager operates as an autonomous AI agent that learns from locally generated interaction ex-perience and makes its spectrum decisions locally, while coordination across agents is achieved through periodic fed-erated model aggregation. By replacing centralized experi-ence collection with persistent local replay and model-level coordination, FASCIA enables collaborative learning across interference-coupled networks without pooling their locally generated interaction experience. The proposed framework combines Mellowmax-based local value learning with federated training and proximal regulariza-tion to accommodate heterogeneous and interference-coupled local experience. The resulting FASCIA-FA and FASCIA-FP implementations achieve spectrum-coordination performance close to the centralized CTDE benchmark while consistently outperforming existing distributed methods. Importantly, this behavior extends beyond the network densities represented during training, demonstrating that the learned federated model can generalize to denser interference environments while maintaining effective decentralized spectrum decisions. Overall, the results demonstrate that federated model-level coordination can provide an effective alternative to centralized training for adaptive spectrum coordination. FASCIA retains the benefits of collaborative learning while allowing interac-tion experience to remain at the individual network managers and preserving decentralized execution. These findings support federated agentic AI as a promising approach for scalable and adaptive spectrum coordination in distributed, interference-coupled wireless networks. REFERENCES [1] K.
