## ABSTRACT 

Existing multi-agent collaboration (MAC) methods have primarily explored a 

group thinking collaboration topology, where agents independently generate solu-tions and aggregate their outputs. In this work, we investigate an alternative relay thinking topology, where reasoning ownership is dynamically transferred across agents, allowing subsequent agents to inherit and refine an evolving reasoning tra-jectory. We introduce Multi-Agent Confidence-guided Relay (M ACR ), a frame-work that enables dynamical relay collaboration among agents. The key chal-lenge is determining when reasoning should be relayed and which agent should continue the process. M ACR addresses this challenge through confidence-guided relay triggering and a bidding mechanism that selects the next agent based on its confidence. Experiments on four benchmarks show that M ACR outperforms the strongest single-model strategies in accuracy, e.g., +3.3% on MathVista and 

+3.9% on MMStar, and remains on par with multi-agent strategies while consum-ing 6.0 × less token overhead on HallusionBench and MMStar. Further analysis demonstrates that relay collaboration enables heterogeneous agents to progres-sively integrate complementary insights, combining heterogeneous capabilities across reasoning stages. Our source code is available at GitHub.

Background. Large Language Models (LLMs) demon-strate impressive reasoning capabilities (Zhang et al., 2025a; Xu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026c). However, when solving complex problems, a single model might encounter intermediate reasoning bottlenecks that limit further progress (Shojaee et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b). Multi-Agent Collaboration (MAC) has emerged as an effective approach to address such chal-lenges by enabling multiple models to interact and exploit complementary capabilities (Jin et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b; Bajoria et al., 2026; Tran et al., 2025; Ferrag et al., 2026; Abou Ali et al., 2025; Gupta et al., 2025). Exist-ing studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of MAC in various domains, e.g., mathematical reasoning (Qian et al., 2025; Davoudi et al., 2025), visual question an-swering (Wang et al., 2026c;a), and multi-step decision making (Weng et al., 2025; Nourzad et al., 2025). 

Motivation. Despite their diversity, most MAC meth-ods follow a group thinking collaboration pattern (Janis, 1982), where multiple agents contribute reasoning trajectories in parallel and integrate informa-tion through interaction or aggregation (Li et al., 2024; Veluri et al., 2024; Becker et al., 2026) ( cf .Fig. 1a). This pattern enables agents to contribute parallel perspectives and reach a synthesized solu-tion. However, collaboration among intelligent systems can take multiple forms. Cognitive science

Methodology. Realizing relay collaboration requires addressing two questions: when should rea-soning ownership be transferred, and which agent should continue the reasoning process. We use model confidence as a signal for these decisions, motivated by previous studies showing that con-fidence estimates can provide information about reasoning reliability (Xu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b; Farquhar et al., 2024). Moreover, we also observe in Fig. 4 that correct and incorrect trajec-tories are separable in confidence. However, confidence values derived from heterogeneous models are not directly comparable due to differences in model architectures, vocabulary spaces, and prob-ability distributions ( cf . Fig. 2). To address this, we propose the Multi-Agent Confidence-guided 

Relay (M ACR ). M ACR begins by extracting local confidence across a heterogeneous model pool using a sliding window for confidence calibration, reducing the Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) F

value from 13 .31 to 1.01 (cf . Fig. 2). On this basis of comparable confidence across heterogeneous models, M ACR monitors the leading model’s calibrated confidence and interrupts generation to ini-tiate bidding once that model shows low confidence, relaying generation rights to the model with the highest confidence. Besides deciding when and to whom to relay, M ACR must also determine which model should start the task. To this end, it initializes the leading model via an initial bidding round, selecting the model with the highest calibrated confidence to start the task. These steps together constitute M ACR ’s online “initiation–monitoring–bidding–relay” loop ( cf . Fig. 3). 

Contributions. First, we offer a new perspective on MAC patterns by drawing on relay thinking ,where a reasoning trajectory is progressively refined and synthesized as it is relayed across heteroge-neous models. Second, we develop M ACR , a confidence-guided relay framework that calibrates con-fidence across heterogeneous models and jointly determines model initiation and reasoning hand-offs through bidding. Third, we reveal two empirical properties underlying effective heterogeneous collaboration: models exhibit complementary strengths across reasoning stages, while reasoning bottlenecks emerge at different stages across tasks. We hope these findings motivate dynamic model scheduling that adapts both reasoning ownership and relay timing throughout inference. 

Results and Analysis. Experiments on four multimodal benchmarks show that M ACR outperforms single-model strategies in accuracy, e.g., +3.3% on MathVista and +3.9% on MMStar, and remains on par with multi-agent strategies while consuming 6.0 × fewer tokens on HallusionBench and MM-Star, respectively. Qualitative analysis reveals that M ACR integrates the isolated cues of single mod-els into a global reasoning chain( cf . §3.3). Furthermore, our analyses confirm that heterogeneous models in M ACR are complementary, both across their capabilities ( cf . Fig. 6a) and across reasoning stages( cf . Fig. 6b), and that they are capable of localizing task-agnostic bottlenecks( cf . Fig. 6c).

## 3 EXPERIMENT 

3.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Datasets. To evaluate M ACR across diverse dimensions, we adopt four benchmarks. MMStar (Chen et al., 2024) evaluates comprehensive multi-modal capabilities across 6 abilities and 18 tasks. Hallu-sionBench (Guan et al., 2024) diagnoses the visual and knowledge hallucinations. LogicVista (Xiao et al., 2024) assesses structured reasoning in visual contexts, covering inductive, deductive, numer-ical, spatial, and causal logic. MathVista (Lu et al., 2024) evaluates joint visual-mathematical reasoning across geometry, charts, and word problems. 

Baselines. We compare M ACR against single-model and multi-agent strategies. The model pool consists of Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2025a), InternVL3.5-8B (Wang et al., 2025b), and 

MiniCPM-V-4.5-8B (Yu et al., 2026). 

• Single-Model Strategies. We evaluate standard input–output prompting ( IO ), where the model directly outputs the solution and answer from the given images and question; CCoT (Mitra et al., 2024) prompts the LLM to generate a scene graph capturing objects, attributes, and their relation-ships first, and then uses that scene graph to produce the final response; and DDCoT (Zheng et al., 2023) decomposes complex problems into actionable sub-tasks and then produces the final answer. 

• Multi-Agent Strategies. We benchmark against two multi-agent strategies: Majority Vote (MV ) (Wang et al., 2023), establishing consensus across independent model generations, and 

H-DMAD (Liu et al., 2025), a diverse multi-agent collaboration framework that assigns different reasoning strategies ( CoT (Zheng et al., 2023), CCoT , and DDCoT ) to different agents, encouraging complementary perspectives to break the fixed mental set, and fuses their outputs into a final answer. 

Implementation Details. We set the sliding window size w = 16 and the confidence threshold τ =

−0.3 via parameter search on 600 randomly sampled instances from RealWorldQA (X.AI, 2024), which are disjoint from all evaluation sets. All evaluations are conducted under identical settings (e.g., temperature). To ensure reliability and rule out the influence of generation randomness, we conduct all evaluations with three distinct random seeds. We report accuracy as the mean ± standard deviation, and additionally report token usage along with the Incremental Benefit per Cost (IBC), defined as ∆Acc /∆Tokens relative to each single model’s IO baseline (Aggarwal et al., 2024). 3.2 QUANTITATIVE COMPARISON RESULTS 

The results demonstrate that M ACR consistently surpasses single-model strategies in accuracy while remaining comparable to multi-agent strategies with minimal token overhead ( cf . Table 1). M ACR 

yields substantial accuracy gains over the strongest single-model strategies, boosting performance by +3.30% on MathVista (78.00% vs. 74.70%) and +3.87% on MMStar (72.00% vs. 68.13%). It secures these enhancements while maintaining commensurate or reduced token overhead. 3.4.1 CAPABILITY ROUTING ANALYSIS 

To validate the necessity of bidding, we analyze the cross-model transition matrix ( cf . Fig. 6a). The row-normalized transition probabilities reveal bidirectional relay dynamics. While MiniCPM-V 4.5 

has the highest self-transition rate (59.7%), it still yields dominance to InternVL 3.5 or Qwen3 V Lin 40.3% of its bids. Furthermore, bidirectional exchanges between peers are frequent. InternVL 3.5 transfers to Qwen3 VL (22.4%), while Qwen3 VL returns control (26.9%). This indicates that no single model maintains absolute dominance. MiniCPM-V 4.5 , InternVL 3.5 , and Qwen3 VL , respectively, compared with M ACR ’s 72.4%. This shows that simulated persona diversity fails to match true architectural complementarity. 

Temperature-Decoded Pattern (TDH ) simulates intra-model diversity by elevating the sampling temperature during the bidding phase, generating three distinct reasoning paths. The trajectory that maximizes local confidence is then adopted and concatenated to the context. TDH yields 66.6%, 68.8%, and 63.6% for MiniCPM-V 4.5 , InternVL 3.5 , and Qwen3 VL , underperforming M ACR . This shows that sampling strategies cannot substitute for complementary heterogeneity. The results indicate homogeneity leads to performance degradation. Consistent with recent find-ings (Bertalaniˇ c & Fortuna, 2026), homogeneous pools lack intrinsic diversity and thus cannot pro-duce cognitive diversity. Conformity tendency may drive them to converge on a flawed consensus. 3.4.6 LEXICAL PROFILING OF REASONING BOTTLENECKS 

To semantically characterize models’ reasoning bottlenecks, we analyze the lexical distribution of tokens generated immediately preceding a bidding trigger (cf. Fig. 7). The resulting distributions show distinct lexical patterns across models. InternVL predominantly falters around analytical ev-idence extraction (e.g., analyze, examine, identify ), MiniCPM-V around spatial localization and at-tribute binding (e.g., position, top, left, large ), and Qwen3-VL around causal deduction (e.g., since, therefore, suggesting ). Despite these differences, all models share a universal hesitation signature, frequently outputting stall tokens (e.g., wait, look, check ). This lexical heterogeneity aligns with the complementarity we observed (cf. §3.4.1), suggesting that different models tend to hesitate at different stages and motivating M ACR ’s design of matching each bottleneck with its optimal model. 

## 4 RELATED WORK 

Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) improves performance by leveraging interactions among LLMs. Ex-isting MAD methods are broadly categorized into two streams. The first category is Collaboration Workflow Optimization (Hong et al., 2024; Maiga et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024; Pham et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Zhang & Xiong, 2025). These methods structure multi-agent col-laboration to resolve tasks, e.g., orchestrating role-based coordination pipelines (Hong et al., 2024; Maiga et al., 2025), communicating via continuous embeddings (Pham et al., 2024), and optimiz-8Preprint (a) InternVL 3.5 (b) MiniCPM-V 4.5 (c) Qwen3 VL 

Figure 7: Lexical distribution of three models. ing communication topologies or multi-perspective reward frameworks (Li et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Zhang & Xiong, 2025). The second category is Failure Diagnosis and Interven-tion (Wynn et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025; Cemri et al., 2026; Choi et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2025b). These methods diagnose and mitigate collaborative limitations, e.g., quantifying sycophancy and social conformity to prevent premature convergence (Wynn et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025), establish-ing taxonomies for system misalignments (Cemri et al., 2026), and delineating theoretical bounds of debate under test-time scaling (Choi et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2025b). Despite their impressive per-formance, these methods are all built around the topology of group thinking , where models produce complete responses that are aggregated after generation. Unlike methods relying on rigid debate turns, M ACR monitors confidence in real time and relays generation rights to the most confident candidate when a model struggles. 

Uncertainty and Confidence in LLMs provide signals for evaluating confidence and guiding rea-soning. These approaches are broadly divided into two main streams. The first category is Uncer-tainty Quantification and Decomposition (Wang et al., 2025a; Jayasekera et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2025; Amayuelas et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026c). They decompose and estimate uncertainty to evalu-ate confidence or trigger behaviors, establishing bounds or benchmarks for uncertainties (Jayasek-era et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025a), inducing uncertainty awareness through query or tuning (Feng et al., 2025; Amayuelas et al., 2024), and extracting token-level logit margins to dynamically trigger interleaved multimodal reasoning (Liu et al., 2026c). The second category is Confidence-Guided Collaboration and Reinforcement (Zhu et al., 2026; Ai et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026; Pappu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Choi et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2026b; Liu et al., 2026a; Park et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). They manage uncertainty within multi-agent frameworks, e.g., modulating belief up-dates through confidence scores (Zhu et al., 2026; Ai et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026), mitigating behavioral uncertainties like hallucination, compromise, or bias during debates (Pappu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Choi et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2026b), and optimizing collaborative policies via trans-lating confidence into reward signals (Liu et al., 2026a; Park et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). Despite quantifying uncertainties and optimizing collaborative policies, these methods evaluate sequence-level outputs and overlook the comparability of confidence across heterogeneous models. Unlike these methods, we first identify the incomparability of confidence across heterogeneous mod-els and provide a calibration method for it. Building on this, M ACR calibrates local confidence at the window level rather than sequence level, monitoring mid-generation uncertainty and executing cross-model relays the moment a local reasoning bottleneck is detected. 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduce M ACR , transitioning multi-agent collaboration from group thinking mode to relay thinking mode. Addressing incomparable confidence across heterogeneous models, M ACR cali-brates local confidence, enabling real-time cross-model bidding. Monitoring the leading model, MACR interrupts generation upon detecting reasoning bottlenecks, transferring generation rights to the most confident model. Experiments across four multi-modal benchmarks demonstrate that MACR outperforms all existing single-model baselines in accuracy, while achieving results com-parable to existing multi-agent baselines with 6.0 × less token overhead.
