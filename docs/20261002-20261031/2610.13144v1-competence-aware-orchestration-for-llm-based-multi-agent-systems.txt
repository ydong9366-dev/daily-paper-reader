# ABSTRACT 

LLM-based multi-agent systems (MAS) have emerged as a promising paradigm for enhancing complex reasoning through role specialization and collaboration, yet a key challenge remains: how can collaboration be adapted to a backbone’s com-petence on the current query? This problem matters because more collaboration is not always better—richer coordination may help on difficult cases, but it also incurs additional token cost and redundant interactions. We propose a two-stage competence-aware framework for automatic MAS orchestration. It first models the LLM backbone’s task-conditioned competence for the current query, and then uses this signal to adapt role activation and the communication graph. Across six benchmarks, our method achieves an average score of 93.81%. Experiments with six LLM backbones of varying competence levels further show that our method adjusts collaboration according to the inferred competence: for higher-competence backbones, it improves average accuracy from 87.22 to 89.97 while reducing to-ken cost by 36.26%; for lower-competence backbones, it yields a further average gain of 4.58 points over existing methods. The same trend also holds on unseen backbones. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have greatly expanded the use of Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) for reasoning, code generation, and complex problem solving (Hong et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). Related progress in prompting, search, self-refinement, ensembling, and text-based optimization has further improved LLM reasoning and adaptation (Nori et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023; Yuksekgonul et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024). By assigning specialized roles and coordinating their interactions, LLM-based MAS can outperform direct single-agent reasoning (Du et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023). However, their effectiveness depends critically on collaboration topology and communication pattern: different tasks, and even different instances, may benefit from different forms and scales of collaboration (Zhang et al., 2025c; Yue et al., 2025; Dang et al., 2026). Moreover, more collaboration is not always better: richer role specialization and denser communication may help on difficult cases, but also introduce additional token cost, latency, and redundant interactions (Zhang et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025b). This motivates a competence-aware perspective on automatic MAS construction: 

How can collaboration be adapted to a backbone’s competence on the current query? 

Recent studies have moved from manually designed pipelines and hand-crafted role-based collabo-ration patterns (Hong et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024) to frameworks that automatically optimize or dynamically generate multi-agent structures (Liu et al., 2023; Zhuge et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025d;c;a; Yue et al., 2025; Dang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025a). By generating role configurations and communication topologies from task descriptions, query content, candidate roles, or runtime feedback, these methods make MAS increasingly task-aware and adaptive (Zhang et al., 2025d; Dang et al., 2026). Yet the main focus has been on organizing agent collaboration around task semantics, while relatively less attention has been paid to explicitly conditioning its scale and structure on the backbone’s competence for the current query. We argue that the utility of collaboration is not fixed: it depends on the backbone’s standalone capability, the difficulty of the query, and the coordination overhead introduced by the system. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We design a competence modeling mechanism that combines a reusable backbone capa-bility fingerprint with query-specific task information to derive a backbone-aware, query-conditioned competence representation. • We develop a competence-aware orchestration method that uses this representation to configure the multi-agent structure at two levels—role activation and communication graph generation—adapting team composition and coordination topology to both the backbone and the current query. • Experiments across multiple benchmarks and backbones show that our method improves overall performance, reduces collaboration token usage on strong backbones, and brings larger gains on weaker backbones, with the same trend also observed on unseen backbones. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Static multi-agent collaboration and design. Early LLM-based multi-agent systems mainly relied on manually designed collaboration templates, including sequential pipelines and debate-style communication Du et al. (2024). Meanwhile, reasoning can also be improved within a single model through methods such as chain-of-thought Wei et al. (2022) and self-consistency Wang et al. (2022). To reduce manual engineering in multi-agent systems, subsequent work began to automate structure design, including workflow optimization methods such as AF LOW Zhang et al. (2025d) and DSP Y

Khattab et al. (2024), as well as graph-based approaches such as GPTS WARM Zhuge et al. (2024), MAC NET Qian et al. (2025), A GENT PRUNE Zhang et al. (2025b), and A GENT DROPOUT Wang et al. (2025b). These methods improve system design by searching, optimizing, or pruning collaboration structures before deployment. However, whether hand-crafted or automatically optimized, they remain largely input-independent and follow a learn once, deploy many paradigm. 

Dynamic orchestration. More recent studies move beyond static structures and dynamically organize collaboration at inference time. Rather than reusing one fixed design, these methods instantiate or route agent interaction according to the current query or runtime setting. Representative examples include G-D ESIGNER , which generates query-specific communication graphs Zhang et al. (2025c); M AS ROUTER , which routes topology, roles, and models Yue et al. # 3 METHOD 

3.1 OVERVIEW 

Our method follows a two-stage competence-aware orchestration pipeline. In Stage I, we learn a task-conditioned competence representation from a reusable backbone capability fingerprint and query information. In Stage II, we use this representation to adapt the multi-agent structure at two levels: role activation and communication graph construction. 3.2 STAGE I: C OMPETENCE MODELING 

3.2.1 CAPABILITY FINGERPRINTING 

To support downstream query-level competence modeling, we precompute a reusable capability fingerprint for each LLM backbone using a fixed probe set P. The probes are shared across backbones, organized by capability domain, and ordered within each domain by anchor-calibrated difficulty, yielding a common easy-to-hard scale for direct comparison. Vm can be reused in all subsequent queries. We represent the fingerprint as a binary matrix Vm ∈ { 0, 1}4×12 , where each row corresponds to one capability domain and the columns within each row are ordered from easy to hard. Let

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETTING 

Datasets and Metrics. Following prior work on automatic multi-agent system design and orches-tration (Zhang et al., 2025c), we evaluate our method on six widely used benchmark datasets: MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2020), GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), MultiArith (Roy & Roth, 2015), SVAMP (Patel et al., 2021), AQuA (Ling et al., 2017), and HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021). We report the standard evaluation metric for each dataset, using accuracy for MMLU, GSM8K, MultiArith, SVAMP, and AQuA, and Pass@1 for HumanEval. Dataset statistics are reported in Table 6. 

Baselines. Following prior work (Zhang et al., 2025c), we compare our method with three groups of baselines. The first group consists of single-agent methods , including Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2022) and Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2022). The second group includes multi-agent systems with fixed collaboration patterns , such as Chain, Tree, Complete Graph, and Random Graph (Qian et al., 2025), as well as debate-based interaction instantiated by LLM-Debate (Du et al., 2024). The third group covers multi-agent methods with dynamic or optimized structures , including AgentPrune (Zhang et al., 2025b), AgentDropout (Wang et al., 2025b), MasRouter (Yue et al., 2025), CARD (Wu et al., 2026), and G-Designer (Zhang et al., 2025c). 6Preprint 

Implementation Details We access GPT models via the OpenAI API, primarily using 

gpt-4o-2024-08-06 (GPT-4o) as the target backbone. Unless otherwise specified, all LLM calls in evaluation use a decoding temperature of 0. For multi-agent methods, we adopt explicit agent profiling with a predefined pool of textual role descriptions, following classical configurations in prior LLM-based MAS work (Liu et al., 2023; Zhuge et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2023). We additionally include a summarizer agent to aggregate the dialogue history and produce the final answer. Both task queries and role descriptions are encoded using bge-small-en-v1.5 (Xiao et al., 2023), yielding 384-dimensional embeddings. We train Task-Conditioned Competence Modeling, Role Activation, and Graph-based MAS Generation sequentially offline, keeping the other modules frozen at each stage. Deployment reuses the trained model without further parameter updates. For training the graph-generation module, following Zhang et al. (2025c), we use 40 queries from each dataset for training. We use dataset-specific training and evaluation splits; their definitions and the construction of capability fingerprints and supervision targets are detailed in Appendix A. 4.2 PERFORMANCE COMPARISON

# 5 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we study how to adapt multi-agent collaboration to a backbone’s competence on the current query . We propose a competence-aware framework that learns a task-conditioned representation under success-rate supervision and uses it to guide role activation and communication topology. Experiments across six benchmarks and multiple LLM backbones show that our method improves performance, reduces collaboration cost on stronger backbones, and yields larger gains on weaker ones, with the same trend also observed on unseen backbones. 9Preprint AI USE STATEMENT

A.1 PROBE SET CONSTRUCTION FOR CAPABILITY FINGERPRINTING 

Training-query pool. Following prior work on automatic MAS design and orchestration (Zhang et al., 2025c;b), which uses B′ ∈ { 40 , 80 } training queries per dataset, we adopt the 40-query setting. We randomly sample 40 queries from each of the six benchmark datasets, forming a pool of 240 training-side queries. Probe-set construction and competence-supervision data collection both draw from this pool, without introducing additional distinct task queries. This matches the distinct-query budget, not the offline LLM execution budget. 12 Preprint Table 5: Domain assignment used in probe-set construction.

Dataset-specific splits. The dataset loader uses the MMLU development split for training queries and the validation split for evaluation queries. For the other five benchmarks, including HumanEval, fixed dataset-specific index files define the training and evaluation subsets. The training and evaluation index sets in these files are disjoint, and the evaluation loader selects only the designated evaluation indices. 

Design goals. We construct the probe set to satisfy three requirements. First, it should provide clear difficulty resolution , so that backbones of different competence levels can be meaningfully separated rather than collapsing to similar outcomes on the same probe set. Second, it should provide multi-domain coverage , so that the resulting capability fingerprint reflects multiple capability dimensions instead of only one narrow skill type. Third, it should remain compact , so that one-time fingerprint registration incurs only limited overhead. 

Anchor-based difficulty calibration. To obtain a stable and interpretable difficulty scale, we evaluate four anchor backbones with progressively stronger standalone capability— 

Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct , Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct , GPT-4o , and GPT-5 —on all 240 sampled queries. For each item x, we record the binary pass vector 

s(x) = ( s1, s 2, s 3, s 4), sk ∈ { 0, 1}, (19) where sk = 1 indicates that the k-th anchor solves x correctly, and sk = 0 otherwise. We order the four anchors from weaker to stronger and retain only items whose pass patterns are monotone under this ordering. Intuitively, if a weaker anchor succeeds while a stronger anchor fails, the item is not suitable for reliable difficulty calibration and is therefore discarded as ambiguous. We additionally remove all-fail items (0 , 0, 0, 0) , since such items do not help locate the capability range of the anchors and may be confounded by ambiguity, annotation noise, or excessively difficult instances. The remaining monotone pass patterns define four relative difficulty levels, ranging from easiest to hardest: items solved by all four anchors (1 , 1, 1, 1) , items solved by all but the weakest anchor 

(0 , 1, 1, 1) , items solved only by the two strongest anchors (0 , 0, 1, 1) , and items solved only by the strongest anchor (0 , 0, 0, 1) . This yields an empirically grounded difficulty scale shared across candidate backbones.

Balanced sampling and final probe set. Combining the four domains with the four difficulty levels yields 16 domain–difficulty cells. We sample 3 items from each cell, resulting in a final probe set of 48 items. For each domain, the selected probes are ordered from easy to hard as three items from each difficulty level, yielding 12 probes per domain in total. This gives the 4 × 12 layout used in capability fingerprinting: the four rows correspond to capability domains, and the 12 columns within each row form a shared easy-to-hard scale. A.2 PROXY SUPERVISION DATA CONSTRUCTION FOR TASK -C ONDITIONED COMPETENCE 

MODELING 

The goal of task-conditioned competence modeling is to make the fused representation capture how competent a given LLM backbone is expected to be on the current query. Since such query-level competence is not directly annotated, we construct a proxy supervision signal from empirical task performance. The intuition is straightforward: if a backbone succeeds more frequently on a query across repeated trials, it should be regarded as more competent on that query; conversely, a lower success rate indicates lower competence. We collect this supervision data offline from the same pool of 240 training-side samples used in Appendix A.1. After excluding the 48 samples selected as the probe set, we use the remaining 192 samples to build the proxy supervision data. Thus, the probe set is used only for Capability Fingerprint registration, while the non-probe samples are used to estimate query-level competence targets.
