## Abstract 

LLM-based agents are increasingly used to complete complex, long-horizon tasks without human intervention. To make progress to-ward the task goal, agents need to perform different duties, such as planning and verification, when needed. To understand how sin-gle agents manage these duties during long-horizon execution, we conduct a preliminary study. Our analysis shows that verification has the strongest association with task success, but often occurs late, leaving limited opportunities for correction. We also find that the proportion of duty-relevant information decreases as records from different duties accumulate in a shared context. These find-ings lead us to examine whether multi-agent systems (MAS) can address these problems by assigning duties to agents with separate contexts. Our analysis shows that this separation alone is insuf-ficient. Agents can lose track of the overall goal and repeat prior investigations. They may also lack necessary background while receiving irrelevant information from others. To maintain goal awareness and coordinate collaboration as task needs change, we propose Goal-Aware Dynamic Multi-Agent Collaboration (GAD). It uses a growing execution graph to organize subtasks and their dependencies. To keep agents focused on their goals, each con-text retains the original instruction and the assigned task’s goal and acceptance conditions. To adapt collaboration and information sharing during execution, agents can divide tasks into subtasks and specify dependencies based on execution evidence. These depen-dencies guide when agents work and which results they receive. Verification and recovery check subtask results and full task com-pletion against their goals, providing feedback for repair when checks fail. Experiments with Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Qwen3.7-Plus on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and SWE-bench Pro Hard-51 show perfor-mance improvements over the evaluated baselines and lower token costs than multi-agent baselines on jointly solved tasks.

## 1 Introduction 

LLM-based agents have shown promising capabilities in completing complex tasks without human intervention, particularly on long-horizon tasks [9, 18]. These tasks can span hundreds of interaction steps, during which agents repeatedly interact with the environ-ment and adapt their actions based on feedback [11, 12]. To guide this process toward successful completion, agents often need to per-form various duties , such as planning, reasoning, verification, and functional responsibilities [1, 5, 8], as needed throughout execution. For example, a web research agent first plans what information to search for [10], then reasons over the retrieved evidence [8], before verifying whether the evidence supports its answer [1]. Analysing these duties is crucial to understand agent trajectories. If an agent cannot allocate and switch duties when needed, it may continue executing outdated plans or leave errors unchecked [12]. There-fore, it is important to understand how agents manage these duties during long-horizon execution. However, allocating duties effectively by a single agent can be challenging. In Section 3.1, we perform a preliminary study to exam-ine how agents manage these duties. Our analysis reveals two key limitations: (1) Among all duties, verification exhibits the strongest association with task success, yet often occurs late in the task pro-cess. Intuitively, early verification of intermediate results can help agents detect and correct errors before they propagate into subse-quent actions. However, agents may continue working on the task without performing the duty of verification, leaving errors unde-tected and limiting opportunities for correction. (2) Agents may struggle to retrieve relevant evidence and prior decisions for the current duty from the extensive accumulated context. Our context analysis shows that as records from different duties accumulate in a shared context, information relevant to the current duty accounts for a decreasing proportion of the interaction history. These two findings motivate us to investigate whether explicitly assigning distinct duties to separate agents using multi-agent systems (MAS) arXiv:2610.39193v1 [cs.AI] 29 Oct 2026 Xu Shen, Lingyong Yan, Shuaiqiang Wang, Jianmin Wu, Zhikai Chen, Jiliang Tang, and Jie Ren 

can address these limitations by maintaining separate contexts and enabling verification when needed. However, the naive adoption of MAS does not resolve the lim-itations of single-agent execution. In Section 3.2, we evaluate six representative multi-agent configurations on duty performance using the same trajectory analysis approach. Our analysis identifies two limitations. (1) Agents can lose track of the overall goal. They might repeat investigations that others have already attempted, spending additional steps without advancing the task. (2) Separate contexts do not automatically improve information management. Agents may lack the background or conditions needed for their duties, while messages from other agents may add irrelevant infor-mation to their contexts. These findings highlight that throughout long-horizon execution, maintaining goal awareness and dynami-cally coordinating duties and information exchange are important. Motivated by these findings, we propose Goal-Aware Dynamic Multi-Agent Collaboration (GAD) for long-horizon tasks. GAD uses a growing execution graph to organize subtasks and guide informa-tion sharing during execution. Each node represents an assigned task, while edges record task decomposition and dependencies. To prevent goal drifting, we retain the original instruction and the assigned task’s goal and acceptance conditions in each agent’s con-text. Each task also retains its goal and the duty to satisfy it as the graph expands. To support dynamic collaboration, agents can divide their assigned tasks into subtasks and specify dependen-cies based on execution evidence. These dependencies guide agent scheduling and dependency-aware context construction to supply relevant results. Verification and recovery check both subtask re-sults and full task completion against their goals and acceptance conditions, providing feedback for repair when checks fail. We conduct experiments on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and SWE-bench Pro Hard-51 with Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Qwen3.7-Plus, demonstrating performance improvements over the evaluated baselines. Further analyses support component effectiveness and show lower token costs than multi-agent baselines on jointly solved tasks. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Long-Horizon Agents 

Recent research improves long-horizon agents through supervised fine-tuning and multi-turn reinforcement learning. The former trains agents on synthetic interaction trajectories [23, 24, 30], while the latter develops decision-making policies through feedback from real or synthesised environments [2, 19, 20, 22]. These approaches improve agent behaviour through training, but extended execu-tion also requires managing accumulated information and tracking task progress. MEM1 jointly learns reasoning and compact mem-ory updates [29]. ReSum summarises interaction histories to sup-port continued search [21], while Context-Folding trains agents to handle subtasks in separate trajectories and retain their sum-maries [16]. LongHorizon-Harness further separates task manage-ment, execution, and auditing to maintain independently checked progress [12]. Our method complements policy training by organ-ising collaboration during execution. It connects agent-proposed subtasks, dependency-guided information sharing, and verification through a growing execution graph, while retaining the task goal. 

## 2.2 Multi-Agent Systems 

Multi-agent collaboration has developed from predefined role pro-cedures towards automatically constructed collaboration struc-tures [7]. GPTSwarm optimises agent prompts and graph connec-tions [31], G-Designer learns task-dependent topologies [27], and AFlow searches executable workflows [28]. Graph-of-Agents fur-ther selects relevant models and structures their exchanges for each query [26]. Alongside structure design, another research direction coordinates agents during execution. Magentic-One uses a central orchestrator to track progress and delegate tasks [6]; Evolving Or-chestration learns agent-selection policies [3], and AOrchestra cre-ates specialised subagents on demand [15]. Self-Organizing Agent Teams also retains collaboration experience as reusable organisa-tional strategies [14]. We examine these concerns through duty allocation and information use, then connect agent-proposed de-composition with dependency-guided execution and verification without a central LLM orchestrator. 

## 3 Preliminary study 

Long-horizon tasks require agents to execute autonomously with limited human intervention and take on different duties as execu-tion needs change. We first examine how single agents allocate these duties and use their shared history to obtain relevant infor-mation (Section 3.1). We then study whether assigning duties to separate agents with their own contexts improves duty organisation and information use (Section 3.2). 

Task setting. We study long-horizon tasks specified by a natural-language instruction 𝐼 and an initial environment state 𝑠 0. An agent uses a policy 𝜋 to select actions based on the instruction and its avail-able context, receiving observations from the environment after each action. A harness manages tool execution, context construc-tion, and termination. In a multi-agent system, it also determines when each agent acts and what information it receives. We evaluate task performance using an external verifier 𝑉 after execution stops; during execution, agents rely on environment observations and their own checks without access to feedback from 𝑉 . We examine how single-agent and multi-agent systems allocate duties and use information over extended interactions.

We summarize the experimental results and observations as follows. 

1. The agent spends the least on verification and leaves it to the end, although verification is highly related to task success. As shown in Figure 1a, verification is the smallest of the main duties (those above 10% of the output), taking at most 14% of the agent’s output. Figure 1b shows that it is also the last duty to appear. It is nearly absent early in a trajectory and peaks in the final segment. The agent thus uses verification mainly as a final verify before it stops, rather than interleaving it with execution. Besides, Figure 1c also shows that the pass rate rises from 36% in the lowest-verification group to 74% in the highest. No passing trajectory skips verification. These results suggest that verification is closely associated with task success, making its limited and late allocation notable. Verifying more is still not sufficient, since about a quarter of the trajectories in the highest group fail. 

2. The context mixes all duties, and the proportion relevant to each duty falls as the trajectory grows. A single agent keeps the history of all its duties in one context. To measure how much of this context the duty of a given step actually needs, we sample steps from every configuration and ask the judge to mark the previous steps whose content this duty requires. A previous step counts as relevant only if its content is needed, not merely because it performs the same type of duty. As shown in Figure 1d, the number of previous steps grows from about 5 to about 30 over a trajectory. In contrast, the number of relevant steps grows only from 1 .4 to 3 .0. As a result, the proportion of relevant context falls from 33% to 14%. Each duty has to find what it needs among the growing amount of content produced for other duties. We argue that giving each duty its own context would potentially prevent this, and verify whether it is effective in Section 3.2. 

Discussion. Our study identifies two key limitations of single-agent execution. First, verification often occurs late, despite its strong association with task success, limiting opportunities for correction. Second, records from different duties accumulate in a shared context, reducing the proportion of information relevant to the current duty. This may make it harder for agents to locate the evidence and prior decisions they need. These findings raise a natural question: Can explicitly assigning different duties to separate agents, each with its own context, improve duty allocation and access to relevant information? 

## 3.2 Can multi-agent systems supply what a single agent lacks? 

In this section, we answer the above question and explain why as-signing duties to separate agents with their own contexts does not by itself resolve the limitations of single-agent execution. Second, we examine whether information sharing provides each agent with the background and results needed for its duties. 1. Fixed roles fail to keep agents aligned with the overall task objective. Multi-agent systems rely on collaboration among agents, yet such collaboration does not necessarily ensure that in-dividual agents remain aligned with the overall task objective. To investigate this issue, we employ a judge to evaluate each agent’s execution history and determine whether each step contributes to progress toward the overall goal. As shown in Figure 2a, multi-agent systems exhibit off-track behavior 2–5 times more frequently than the single agent. Nearly all off-track steps involve repetitive actions that have already been attempted, indicating that agents become trapped in unproductive cycles. This behavior stems from the rigid organization of existing multi-agent systems: agent roles and execu-tion order are predefined before the task is fully understood, while individual agents operate in isolation without monitoring overall task progress. These systems lack the mechanisms to dynamically adapt agent responsibilities based on feedback from task execution. 

2. Agents lack awareness of one another’s information needs, leading to ineffective information exchange. In multi-agent systems, each agent maintains its own context and exchanges messages with other agents to facilitate collaboration. However, we find that 4%–30% of the exchanged messages are irrelevant to their receivers, with systems employing more aggressive message compression exhibiting the highest irrelevance rates (Figure 2b). This inefficiency arises because senders lack awareness of the in-formation needs of their receivers. While message summarization reduces context consumption, it may also discard critical informa-tion required by downstream agents. Furthermore, newly delegated agents often lack sufficient background information to perform their assigned tasks effectively. As shown in Figure 2c, their first two execution steps exhibit failure rates up to 2 .3 times higher than those of subsequent steps. Both observations highlight a funda-mental limitation: existing systems lack mechanisms for agents to identify and communicate their information needs. Effective multi-agent collaboration therefore requires explicitly modeling information dependencies among agents and enabling information exchange based on these dependencies. 

Discussions. The above analysis reveals two critical capabilities missing from existing multi-agent systems for long-horizon tasks. The first is goal awareness , which requires agents to continuously align their actions with the overall task objective and verify their progress toward that objective throughout execution. Existing sys-tems lack mechanisms to monitor overall task progress, allowing agents to deviate from the goal and repeatedly perform unproduc-tive actions. The second is dynamic collaboration , which requires agents to adapt their responsibilities and information exchange based on the evolving needs and dependencies revealed during task execution. Existing systems instead rely on predefined roles and communication structures, preventing agents from performing the right duties at the right time and exchanging the information needed for effective collaboration. Together, goal awareness and dynamic collaboration are essential for multi-agent systems to suc-cessfully execute long-horizon tasks. 

## 4 Method 

As discussed in the previous section, effective long-horizon collabo-ration requires goal awareness to keep local tasks aligned with the overall objective, and dynamic collaboration to adapt the division of duties and information sharing to execution needs. To provide these capabilities, we propose Goal-Aware Dynamic Multi-Agent Collaboration (GAD). Our framework builds on a growing execu-tion graph that starts from the original task and expands through agent-proposed subtasks and dependencies. Throughout expansion, each node retains its goal and the duty to satisfy it. We first present this graph and its execution loop in Section 4.1. We then introduce two supporting mechanisms in Section 4.2: dependency-aware con-text construction supplies relevant information for node execution, while verification and recovery check results and provide feedback for further execution. ## 4.1 The Main Framework: Growing Execution Graph 

To support goal awareness and dynamic collaboration, we use a growing execution graph to organise task goals and dependencies. The graph adapts to emerging execution needs while retaining each node’s goal and the duty to satisfy it. In the rest of this subsection, we first define the graph and explain how execution starts from the root. We then introduce agent-proposed decomposition and the controller’s validation and graph updates. Finally, we describe the execution loop and how it returns to the original task for integration and completion. 

Notations. To represent task goals and their dependencies as col-laboration develops, we maintain an execution graph G𝑡 = (N 𝑡 , E𝑡 ),where 𝑡 indexes scheduling steps. Each node 𝑣 represents an as-signed task and stores its specific goal 𝑔 𝑣 , acceptance conditions 𝐾 𝑣 

that determine whether the goal has been achieved, and an execu-tion status 𝜎 𝑣 . Edges connect a task to its subtasks, or to another task whose verified result it requires. 

Step 1. Starting from the root. Initially, N0 = {𝑣 root }, E0 = ∅,and 𝑔 𝑣 root = 𝐼 . We include full satisfaction of 𝐼 as the root’s ac-ceptance condition and then launch an agent whose initial context contains the instruction, this completion requirement, and the root’s status. The graph has not yet expanded, so the root has no depen-dency results to draw on. Its execution history starts empty and accumulates observations as the agent interacts with the environ-ment. These records guide subsequent actions without requiring a complete workflow in advance. The agent starts by interacting directly with the environment and uses the resulting observations to guide subsequent actions, without constructing a complete work-flow in advance. 

Step 2. On-demand recursive decomposition. As agents exe-cute their assigned tasks, new observations may reveal needs for additional subtasks. To adapt the division of duties to these needs, we allow each executing agent to request decomposition. We de-scribe when the agent makes this decision, what it submits, and how the controller uses the request to update the graph. 

• When to decompose. We use on-demand decomposition to adapt the division of duties to needs that emerge during execution. The agent executing each node decides whether to introduce subtasks, without relying on a central LLM orchestrator or a predefined communication topology. ## 4.2 Supporting Mechanisms: Context Construction and Verification 

To support execution of the growing graph, we need to provide agents with relevant information and assess whether their results satisfy the assigned goals. We introduce two supporting mecha-nisms: dependency-aware context construction supplies the inputs for each node’s execution, while verification and recovery check its results and provide feedback for further execution. We describe these mechanisms in Sections 4.2.1 and 4.2.2, respectively. 

4.2.1 Dependency-Aware Context Construction. As mentioned in Section 4.1, the execution graph determines which nodes can exe-cute next. The agents launched for these nodes also need relevant background and results to continue execution. Our analysis in Section 3.2 shows that agents can lack this information or receive messages unrelated to their needs. We address this gap through two complementary mechanisms: dependency-based sharing provides verified results from declared dependencies, while dependency-free retrieval supplies additional information without requiring explicit dependency links.

## 5 Experiments 

In this section, we first describe the experimental setup in Sec-tion 5.1 and present the main results in Section 5.2. We then conduct ablation studies in Section 5.3. Next, we evaluate how our approach addresses the two limitations of MAS in Section 5.4. Finally, we analyze its computational efficiency in Section 5.5. 

## 5.1 Experimental Setup 

Datasets. We evaluate GAD on two challenging benchmarks, namely Terminal-Bench 2.1 [13] and the Hard-51 subset of the 

SWE-Bench Pro V2 [4]. Specifically, Terminal-Bench 2.1 contains 89 tasks covering diverse terminal operations. Hard-51 contains 51 challenging repository-level software engineering tasks. We report task resolution rates using the benchmark verifiers. For SWE-Bench Pro , we evaluate generated patches in fresh environments following the official protocol. 

Baselines. Our single-agent baselines include the following three methods: ReAct based loop [25], Terminus-KIRA , Terminus-2 . For static multi-agent collaboration, we compare Chain, Star, Full [27], MetaGPT [7], and Mixture-of-Agents [17]. For dynamic collabora-tion, we include Subagents, Magentic-One [6], and LongHorizon-Harness [12] with ReAct and Claude Code backends. 

Implementation details. We use Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Qwen3.7-Plus as the base models. We run tasks in containerised environ-ments, with Docker as the default backend. Our method uses the same base model for node execution and semantic verification. We allow up to three concurrent workers per task and include the seven most recent node execution records in each local context. We retrieve up to six shared results per query. Our method per-mits up to 1,000 tool calls and 2,000 model calls per task, without an aggregate token limit. The wall-clock limit is 18,000 seconds on Terminal-Bench; the available Hard-51 runs use 3,000 or 4,500 seconds. Appendix C provides the detailed settings. 

## 5.2 Main Results 

In this section, we compare GAD with three single-agent harnesses and nine multi-agent systems. Table 1 reports the resolution rates. The main findings are as follows. 

GAD consistently outperforms the single-agent harnesses. 

As shown in Table 1, GAD achieves the highest resolution rate across all benchmarks and base models. Compared with the strongest single-agent harness in each setting, it improves resolution rates on Terminal-Bench 2.1 by 14.6 and 7.8 percentage points un-der Sonnet 4.6 and Qwen3.7-Plus , respectively. The correspond-ing gains on Hard-51 of SWE-Bench Pro are 2 .0% and 1 .9%. The improvements are larger on Terminal-Bench, while the strongest single-agent harness remains competitive on Hard-51. We suggest that these improvements result from separating local task execution from independent verification, so checking does not rely solely on the executing agent’s own duty allocation. Node-specific contexts also let agents continue from relevant records without recovering their task state from the mixed history of all duties. 

GAD achieves higher resolution rates than existing multi-agent designs. As shown in Table 1, GAD exceeds the strongest MAS baseline by 6 .7% and 6 .7% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 , and by 23 .6% and 3 .9% on Hard-51, under two base model, respectively. The baseline results also show that multi-agent designs do not consistently outperform single-agent harnesses, and dynamic de-signs do not always improve over fixed topologies. These observa-tions support our preliminary finding that separating duties across agents alone does not ensure effective collaboration. We suggest that these improvements result from adapting the division of du-ties to execution needs while retaining the original goal and each node’s acceptance conditions. Explicit dependencies also connect task scheduling with the results each agent receives, addressing the missing background and irrelevant information exchange observed in existing MAS. In summary, we suggest that these gains result from on-demand decomposition, which lets agents focus on subtasks revealed dur-ing execution, and dependency-aware context construction, which provides the prior results needed to continue without repeating earlier investigations.

## 5.4 Improvement on the two limitations in MAS 

To examine whether GAD provides the goal awareness and dy-namic collaboration that existing multi-agent systems lack, we audited its trajectories with the procedure of Section 3.2. GAD de-composes seven of the 45 Terminal-Bench 2.1 tasks. Only these trajectories involve more than one agent, so the analysis uses these seven tasks. All systems run on Sonnet 4.6 .

Agents in GAD stay on the subgoals they are assigned. As shown in Figure 4b, when each execution step is judged against the subgoal assigned to the agent that took it, only 10 .2% of the steps of GAD leave their subgoal. The rate is 14 .9% for subagents and 37 .7% for MetaGPT. We suggest that a subgoal formed from execution gives each agent a clear target, because it carries its own acceptance criteria. Each agent thus keeps its work tied to the goal. 

Agents in GAD start working as soon as they are assigned. 

As shown in Figure 4c, 10 .1% of the first two steps of a newly assigned agent in GAD end in a tool error. Its later steps fail at a similar rate of 9 .8%. In chain, the first two steps fail in 35 .7% of cases, twice its later rate of 17 .6%. We suggest that GAD gives each agent the verified results of the subgoals it depends on. The agent thus starts from what is already known and does not need to explore the environment first. Goal-Aware Dynamic Multi-Agent Collaboration for Long-Horizon Tasks 

## 5.5 Efficiency Analysis 

We measure cost by the tokens a system consumes on a task. The count adds the input and output tokens of every LLM call made by every agent in the system, including the calls GAD makes for verification. The main findings are as follows. GAD solves the same tasks at lower cost. We compare each baseline with GAD on the tasks both systems solve, and report the median token usage. As shown in Figure 4d, on Terminal-Bench 2.1 with Sonnet 4.6 ,GAD needs about 50k tokens per task. Multi-agent systems need 105–170k on the same tasks, and the long-horizon harness needs 2.6M. We suggest that the cost of existing multi-agent systems comes from coordination that they pay regardless of the task. GAD adds structure only when the goal requires it. On simple tasks it runs like a single agent, so its cost matches the single agent and stays below systems that coordinate on every task. Its accuracy gain does not come from spending more computation. 

## 6 Conclusion 

Long-horizon tasks require LLM agents to manage multiple duties throughout repeated interactions with the environment. Our anal-ysis shows that verification has the strongest association with task success but often comes late, while the proportion of duty-relevant information decreases as the context grows. Existing multi-agent systems can lose track of the overall goal, and context separation alone does not ensure effective information sharing. We propose GAD to maintain goal awareness and organize collaboration as execution reveals new needs. It retains the original instruction and assigned task goals and acceptance conditions in each agent’s con-text. A growing execution graph guides scheduling and information sharing as agents divide tasks and specify dependencies during ex-ecution. Verification and recovery check both subtask results and full task completion, providing feedback for repair. Experiments with Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Qwen3.7-Plus on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and SWE-bench Pro Hard-51 show gains of up to 6.7 percentage points over the strongest baseline.

## B Pseudo-code for GAD 

Algorithm 1 presents the core workflow of GAD in sequential form. Lines 1–2 initialize the graph, shared results, environment, and root task. At each iteration, Lines 4–9 update node states, check stopping conditions, select a ready node, and generate an action from its local context. Lines 10–18 handle environment execution, decompo-sition, and retrieval. An accepted decomposition expands the graph, while a rejected request returns feedback. Lines 19–22 check node completion and either publish a verified result or retain feedback for repair. Line 24 returns a node to READY if it remains ACTIVE .The loop stops when the root passes verification, the budget runs out, or no ready nodes remain. Only root verification establishes task completion. In concurrent execution, the controller waits for running agents before treating an empty frontier as a stopping condition.
