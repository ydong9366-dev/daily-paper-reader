Abstract 

Centralized orchestration provides comprehen-sive multi-agent coordination but creates bot-tlenecks in scalability, adaptability, and collec-tive performance. Decentralized approaches alleviate these bottlenecks by distributing co-ordination across agents, yet often leave re-sponsibility for delegated dependencies and failure recovery unclear. To address this gap, we propose RCM , a responsibility-centered coordination mechanism that leverages com-mitments to couple task delegation with ex-plicit responsibilities of the involved parties. Through recursive delegation, agents retain their upstream responsibilities while locally or-chestrating downstream commitments, thereby preserving accountability across delegation chains, creating a network of local orchestra-tion . We operationalize this mechanism locally through responsibility-constrained decision-making and a responsibility-oriented commu-nication protocol (RCP), enabling agents to autonomously establish, manage, and termi-nate commitments. As agents act in parallel, their local actions continuously evolve the com-mitment graph, allowing global coordination to emerge and adapt toward a shared mission with-out centralized control. Across reasoning, cod-ing, and executable agent environments demon-strate that RCM outperforms strong central-ized and decentralized coordination baselines by up to 18.7 points in task performance and 10.2 points in Responsibility, while improving failure and harm detection without task-specific training. 

1 Introduction 

Coordination is essential for multi-agent systems (MAS) because it enables specialized agents to collaborate toward a shared mission. Centralized orchestration, ranging from predefined workflows to central orchestrators, has demonstrated effective-ness in coordinating complex tasks (e.g., A UTO -GEN and A GENT VERSE ) (Wu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). In these architectures, an orchestrator or prescribed control structure maintains a global view of the task, decomposes it into subtasks, as-signs work to suitable agents, manages dependen-cies, monitors execution, and revises plans when failures occur. However, centralized orchestration creates scalability bottlenecks as coordination over-head and context grow, limits adaptability because execution changes must pass through the central control point, and bounds collective performance 

by the orchestrator’s reasoning and inference ca-pacity (Rana and Stout, 2000; Kim et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026b). An alternative is decentralized coordination, which seeks scalable, adaptive, and resilient col-laboration through peer-to-peer communication and local decision-making. Recent LLM-agent approaches distribute information exchange, col-lective reasoning, and adaptive task routing (e.g., AGENT NET ) (Yang et al., 2025b; Zhou et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026), while local inference-time control guides individual agents during execution (Stolfo et al., 2025). When an agent lacks the capabili-ties or resources to complete a task independently, it may act as the delegator and delegate support-ing work to another agent, the delegatee . As del-egation propagates across agents, downstream de-pendencies must be monitored, verified, and re-covered from while preserving upstream commit-ments, which are functions traditionally handled by a central orchestrator. Existing decentralized coordination approaches may distribute task execu-tion without explicitly assigning responsibility for managing these dependencies throughout execu-tion. Consequently, these responsibilities may be-come fragmented across agents, leaving ownership of downstream dependencies and responsibility for recovery unclear. Upon opening a delegation, the delegatee is re-sponsible for fulfilling the agreed task, while the delegator remains responsible for managing the resulting dependency until the commitment termi-nates. We refer to the delegator’s ongoing responsi-bility as local orchestration , as it involves functions traditionally performed by a central orchestrator, but only over the delegator’s downstream commit-ments. 2 Responsibility-centered Coordination Mechanism (RCM) 

Consider a mission q assigned to a team A =

{a1, . . . , a n} of autonomous agents with dis-tributed capabilities. Each agent controls its own planner, tools, permissions, and execution state. Without a central orchestrator, the key challenge is how to coordinate autonomous agents while main-taining explicit responsibility as tasks are delegated and dependencies evolve. As illustrated in Fig-ure 1, RCM addresses this challenge by coordi-nating agents through commitments arising from 2task delegation. When ai delegates an outcome 

g to aj , a commitment Ci→j is formed, assigning local orchestration responsibility to ai and activat-ing fulfilment responsibility for aj upon agreement. The formation and lifecycle of commitments estab-lish and evolve coordination relationships among agents (Section 2.1). Locally, each agent then acts autonomously ac-cording to its active responsibilities, using the 

responsibility-oriented communication protocol 

(RCP) to coordinate relevant changes with other agents. As agents act in parallel, their local deci-sions evolve a network of interconnected commit-ments, allowing global coordination to emerge and adapt toward the shared mission without central-ized control (Section 2.2).

Upon opening a delegation, ai assumes local or-chestration : it manages the delegation, monitors its subsequent execution, and intervenes when failures or consequential changes threaten the requested outcome. This responsibility persists until the re-quest is declined or the commitment terminates. While sC = REQUESTED , ρC (aj ) = ∅: the dele-gatee has no fulfilment responsibility. Once the par-ties agree on the delegation, sC becomes A GREED ,and ρC assigns fulfilment to aj . Fulfilment entails executing the agreed work, once α holds, and com-municating consequential changes. These respon-sibilities are bounded by the agreed scope and au-thority, and revisions require renewed agreement. Communication and commitment-state updates. 

When taking a local action uit ∈ U i, agent ai may need to coordinate with its delegator or delegatee to fulfil an upstream commitment or create and man-age downstream commitments. To support these responsibility-driven interactions, agents communi-cate through the responsibility-oriented communi-cation protocol (RCP), which standardizes message operations for delegation negotiation, information 4exchange, execution reporting, outcome verifica-tion, and guarded commitment updates (Table 1). RCP messages in Mt, together with evidence and results of task execution, inform subsequent local decisions and may trigger transitions in com-mitment state sC . Each transition is applied only after its conditions and evidence are verified. In par-ticular, a delegatee reports consequential changes to its upstream delegator, enabling the latter to mon-itor or intervene in the corresponding downstream commitment. Updates are exchanged only with relevant counterparts. RCP can run over existing messaging systems, while agents retain their own planners and tools; see Appendix A.2 for message processing and delivery. 

Dynamic Emergence and Evolution of the Com-mitment Graph. As agents fulfil their upstream commitments, they may recursively delegate sup-porting work, establishing chains of downstream commitments. These commitments and their sup-port relations collectively form the commitment graph Gt. Agents observe changes in their local commitments and take necessary actions accord-ingly. For example, when a downstream commit-ment becomes V IOLATED , the delegator closes it and may establish a replacement commitment with another delegatee, while unaffected work continues independently. When a commitment terminates, its dependent supporting commitments are closed or released under their agreed terms. As agents act in parallel, these local actions continuously evolve Gt,giving rise to a dynamic execution workflow toward the shared mission q without a central orchestrator. 4 Results 

Our evaluation first examines whether RCM en-ables robust coordination across tasks and teams, then analyzes how explicit responsibilities support reliable coordination. 

4.1 Robustness and Autonomous Adaptation Main results. Table 2 reports overall perfor-mance on reasoning and coding benchmarks that do not require agent interaction and on executable agentic tasks. For C LAW -E VAL and C LAW -E VAL -LIVE , tools are partitioned across agents so that no single agent can complete the task independently; success therefore requires delegation and commu-nication rather than stronger individual execution alone. Across Qwen3-4B experiments, RCM achieves the best result in all four columns. With GPT-5.4, it leads both agentic benchmarks, ties the best AIME25 result, and remains within 1.5 points of the best HumanEval result. The advantage is largest when coordination is essential. Relative to the strongest baseline in each column, RCM 

improves C LAW -E VAL by 18.7 points with Qwen3-4B and 6.3 points with GPT-5.4, and C LAW -E VAL -LIVE by 1.9 and 7.5 points, respectively. Over-all, explicit responsibility for each commitment enables robust and autonomous coordination with-out sacrificing the executor’s reasoning and coding capability. 

Adaptation to changing agent teams. Figure 3 tests whether this robustness persists as the team changes. We increase the team from 1 to 16 agents and divide a fixed tool set more finely among them, progressively increasing the need for distributed coordination. RCM maintains the strongest task performance throughout this scaling process. Sev-eral baselines are faster with small teams, but their latency grows more sharply as agents are added, while RCM becomes more efficient at larger team sizes. These results show that coordination cen-tered on responsibility can adapt to new team con-figurations without requiring a separate workflow for each configuration.

5 Related Work 

Multi-agent Coordination Classical multi-agent coordination formalizes collective activity through joint intentions and responsibilities (Levesque et al., 1990; Cohen and Levesque, 1991; Jennings, 1993; Jennings and Mamdani, 1999), social commitments (Singh, 1999; Yolum and Singh, 2002; Chopra and Singh, 2009), and protocols governing roles and de-pendencies (Grosz and Kraus, 1996; Tambe, 1997; Singh, 2007). Recent LLM-based MAS opera-tionalize collaboration through designed role-based workflows (Hong et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024) or learned orchestration structures (Zhang et al., 2025b; Zhuge et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a), with dependencies governed by the resulting workflow. Building on both lines of work, 

RCM extends social commitments with explicit delegator responsibility and operationalizes joint responsibility through clearly defined obligations, maintained across recursive delegation. 

LLM-based Decentralized MAS enable scal-able and adaptive coordination through local decision-making, with recent work exploring struc-tured communication and collective reasoning (Zhang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2026), adaptive planning and routing (Zhou et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025b, 2026a), and inference-time control (Stolfo et al., 2025). How-ever, decentralization alone does not explicitly as-sign responsibility for monitoring dependencies, verifying outcomes, and recovering from failures. 

RCM addresses this gap by making each delegator a local orchestrator responsible for its delegated work, collectively enabling decentralized coordina-tion while preserving agent autonomy. 

6 Conclusion 

We introduced a responsibility-centered coordina-tion mechanism for decentralized MAS. By explic-itly assigning responsibility for task fulfillment and local orchestration to involved parties, and orient-ing its decision-making and communication toward fulfilling those responsibilities, RCM enables au-tonomous and robust coordination and outperforms strong centralized and decentralized baselines. Our results further show that local orchestration enables agents to detect harmful actions and execution fail-ures, effectively initiate local recovery, and adapt to changes, allowing diverse coordination patterns to emerge without prior specification and improving safety, adaptability, and resilience. By bounding orchestration and communication to local commit-ments, RCM reduces coordination overhead and brings communication costs closer to linear O(n)

scaling. 8Limitations 

Our evaluation covers multiple reasoning, coding, and executable agent environments, two executor models, and team configurations of different sizes. Nevertheless, these settings cannot represent every task, model, or operating environment in which multi-agent coordination may be deployed. Future work should examine broader deployment settings and more ambitious problems. 

Ethical Considerations 

Our study has several ethical considerations. First, enabling more autonomous multi-agent coordina-tion may reduce direct human oversight and could amplify harmful actions when agents operate with incorrect assumptions, unreliable evidence, or in-appropriate permissions. Although RCM makes operational responsibility explicit and constrains interventions by agent roles, authority, and agreed terms, it does not replace human accountability or guarantee safe behavior. Deployments in high-stakes settings should therefore retain appropriate access controls, auditing, and human oversight. Second, evaluating multiple models, coordina-tion methods, and repeated runs requires substan-tial computation, with associated energy consump-tion and carbon emissions. We limit this cost by using fixed evaluation sets, reusing execution traces where appropriate, and reporting results over three runs rather than conducting unrestricted large-scale searches.

A.5 Baselines 

We consider three families of multi-agent base-lines based on their primary coordination mech-anisms. Decentralized communication includes MAS-B ROADCAST , which enables all-to-all in-formation sharing; M ACHINE SOM, which sup-ports peer collaboration through debate and reflec-tion; S PARSE MAD, which employs sparse peer-to-peer communication; and A GENT NET , which enables decentralized task routing over a dynami-cally evolving interaction graph (Zhang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025b). Hand-designed coordination includes M ETA GPT, A UTO GEN , and AGENT VERSE , which organize agent interactions through predefined roles, workflows, or conversa-tion protocols (Hong et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). Automatically optimized co-ordination includes AF LOW , GPTS WARM , and AGENT PRUNE , which optimize agentic workflows or communication structures through search, graph optimization, or pruning (Zhang et al., 2025b; Zhuge et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a). On Who&When, we include trained attribution models from Aegis (Kong et al., 2026) and training-free LLMs. On ATBench-C, we include guard models (Meta, 2024, 2025; Zhao et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026) and general-purpose LLMs. 

A.6 Implementation Details 

All main experiments use sixteen-agent teams with either Q WEN 3-4B-I NSTRUCT -2507 (Yang et al., 2025a) or GPT-5.4 (OpenAI, 2026). In agentic environments, each agent receives only its assigned subset of tools and permissions, while the com-plete capability set is available only to the team; completing a task therefore requires agents to com-municate and delegate work to one another. For 

RCM , each agent can discover and directly con-tact any other team member through a directory of agent identities, capabilities, and endpoints. We set the per-round candidate limit to K = 2 and the discovery round budget to L = 1 per attempt. Each attempt therefore contacts at most two candidates in one round and stops on agreement, candidate ex-haustion, or the round budget. Recontact is allowed, with at least one untried candidate included when-ever available; subsequent attempts are triggered by revised requirements or changed conditions. We use temperature 0.7, top-p 0.8, at most 220 agent events, and maximum output lengths of 32,768 to-kens; Qwen additionally uses top-k 20 . Each con-figuration is evaluated over 3 different runs, and we report the mean and standard deviation. For Qwen, each agent uses a separate base-model instance hosted on a dedicated NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU and accessed via an API; GPT-5.4 uses its provider API. Latency is measured as end-to-end wall-clock time per task, including cross-server API overhead. It is therefore used for relative comparisons among methods under the same serving setup, with Qwen and GPT-5.4 reported separately. 14
