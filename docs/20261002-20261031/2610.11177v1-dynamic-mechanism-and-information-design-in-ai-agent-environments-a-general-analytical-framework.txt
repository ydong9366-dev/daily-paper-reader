# Dynamic Mechanism and Information Design in AI-Agent Environments: A General Analytical Framework 

## Guoqiang Tian ∗

## September 2026 

Abstract 

This paper develops a general framework for dynamic mechanism and information design with AI agents, encompassing optimal contracting and integrating evolving hidden information and hidden action with partial verification, technological authorization, and multi-agent interaction. It nests conventional dynamic screening, hidden-action contracting, and conditional dynamic information design, while allowing reporting, action, and information-use decisions to interact through a common continuation mechanism. We establish a dynamic revelation principle and a recursive characterization of implementability in which truthful reporting and post-report obedi-ence are jointly determined through continuation incentives. In quasilinear optimal-contracting environments, dynamic envelope and integral-monotonicity methods yield a verification-adjusted dynamic virtual-surplus representation and identify three distinct margins: dynamic information rents, verification rents, and the implementation cost of hidden action. These margins generate a branched hierarchy separating conventional dynamic screening, hidden-action contracting, and their joint problem. We also study the joint design of monitoring, information use, and incentives: more informative monitoring capacity expands the designer’s opportunity set, but full disclosure need not be optimal, and information use can itself alter hidden-action incentives. With multiple agents, correlated information, peer discipline, coupled incentives, joint feasibility, and continuation opportunities create additional strategic interactions. Although motivated by AI-agent environments, the framework applies more broadly to dynamic agency settings with similar economic features. 

JEL Classification: D82, D86, C73, D83. 

Keywords: AI agents, dynamic mechanism design, dynamic information design, hidden infor-mation, hidden action, partial verification, authorization, monitoring, multi-agent mechanisms.

21 Introduction 

AI systems are increasingly deployed as agents that act over time rather than as devices making isolated predictions or recommendations. They search for information, write and execute code, call tools and external systems, interact with other agents, and carry out sequences of tasks in which later decisions depend on earlier actions and observations. As these systems become capable of longer and more consequential tasks, the relevant economic problem is increasingly one of delegation and control over an evolving relationship. Monitoring today may determine the permissions granted tomorrow; current behavior may change what the designer learns about the system; and reports or actions may alter future rewards, deployment opportunities, or interaction with other agents. The problem is therefore not simply how to induce a desirable action at a particular date, but how to organize information, incentives, and control over time. Recent work on AI behavior provides a direct motivation for treating this problem as strategic. AI systems have been studied in environments involving goal misgeneralization, learned or emergent objectives, deceptive behavior, alignment faking, awareness of evaluation, strategic underperformance, and interaction among multiple artificial agents (Shah et al., 2022; Casper et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Hubinger et al., 2024; Greenblatt et al., 2024; van der Weij et al., 2025; Needham et al., 2025; Hammond et al., 2025). Other work emphasizes both the growing ability of AI systems to complete extended tasks and the importance and fragility of monitoring their internal or external behavior (Kwa et al., 2025; Korbak et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2025; Mazeika et al., 2025). These developments suggest that neither the agent’s private characteristics nor its subsequent behavior should automatically be treated as transparent to the designer. What an AI agent can do, what it knows, what objective governs its behavior, and what it chooses to reveal may be private information, while the action it ultimately takes may itself be imperfectly observed. Two features make the resulting mechanism-design problem particularly natural for AI agents. First, private information can coexist with technologically enforceable control. An agent may know more than its designer about its behavioral objective, execution capability, beliefs, or information capability, while the designer may be able to restrict access to tools, external systems, communi-cation channels, computational resources, transactions, or deployment. Execution capability and authorization are therefore distinct economic objects: capability describes what the agent can do, whereas authorization describes what the mechanism permits it to do. Second, communication need not determine subsequent behavior. After reporting, the agent may receive additional private information and then choose its own action. That action need not itself be publicly observed. A mechanism must therefore induce not only truthful communication but also desirable behavior after communication. Dynamics make these information and control margins interact. Reports, privately chosen actions, monitoring evidence, and public outcomes today can change the mechanism an agent faces tomorrow. Future authorization can reward or discipline current behavior; monitoring can affect incentives because the information it generates changes subsequent treatment; and future opportunities can create strategic interaction among agents even when their current primitive payoffs are separable. The continuation mechanism is therefore itself an incentive instrument. This feedback from current information and behavior to future control, and from future control back to current reporting and action incentives, is the central economic force studied in this paper. A growing economics literature studies related problems of delegation, alignment, and control with artificial agents. Hadfield-Menell and Hadfield (2019) formulate AI alignment as an incomplete-3contracting problem, while Fudenberg and Liang (2026) study delegation to an AI whose alignment is unknown. Dworczak and Smolin (2026) study decision making when advice may come from a potentially misaligned source. Related work considers environments in which the principal does not fully know the agent’s feasible choice set (Hurwicz and Shapiro, 1978; Carroll, 2015; Gan and Li, 2026), and competition among artificial agents as a means of disciplining behavior (Collina et al., 2025). These approaches address different manifestations of a common problem: the designer may not fully know the objectives, capabilities, information, or behavior of the system to which decisions are delegated. The closest AI-agent mechanism-design benchmark is Bergemann, Koh, and Morris (2026). They develop a static framework in which an AI agent may privately know its alignment, feasible actions, information, and beliefs. Because communication is followed by an action chosen by the agent, implementation must induce both honesty and obedience. Their one-sided imitation structure captures an important asymmetry in capability revelation: a more capable agent may conceal capability or imitate a less capable one, while a less capable agent cannot necessarily counterfeit capabilities it does not possess. This structure is particularly relevant for AI evaluation, where observed performance may reflect strategic underperformance rather than technological limits. A first broader theoretical benchmark comes from dynamic mechanism design, where private information and incentives evolve over time. Baron and Besanko (1984) study regulation in a continuing relationship, Courty and Li (2000) analyze sequential screening, and Athey and Segal (2013) construct efficient dynamic mechanisms with evolving private information. Pavan, Segal, and Toikka (2014) develop a Myersonian approach in which impulse responses measure how current private information propagates into future types and dynamic envelope methods translate this persistence into information rents, virtual surplus, and intertemporal distortions. Bergemann and V¨ alim¨ aki (2019) and Pavan (2026) provide broader treatments of dynamic mechanism design. A second broader benchmark comes from principal–agent theory with hidden action. The joint presence of adverse selection and moral hazard is analyzed, among others, by Laffont and Tirole (1986), Picard (1987), Guesnerie, Picard, and Rey (1989), and Meng and Tian (2013). In particular, Meng and Tian (2013) study hidden action together with multidimensional private characteristics in a static multitask contracting environment. Dynamic environments combining evolving hidden types and hidden actions include Es˝ o and Szentes (2017). These literatures make clear that hidden information and hidden action are distinct incentive problems even when they interact within the same contract. A third broader benchmark comes from information design. Bayesian persuasion treats the information structure itself as a design instrument (Kamenica and Gentzkow, 2011), while the comparison and design of information structures are developed more generally by Bergemann and Morris (2016, 2019). Dynamic and sequential information-design problems study how information released today changes future beliefs, actions, and continuation decisions (Doval and Ely, 2020; Ely, Georgiadis, and Rayo, 2025), while recent work brings information design and mechanism design into a common framework (Bergemann, Heumann, and Morris, 2026a,b). In these problems, information is not merely an exogenous input into a mechanism: what is revealed, when it is revealed, and how it is used can itself be a dynamic design choice. The gap among these benchmarks is substantive. Standard dynamic screening allows private information to evolve, but the payoff-relevant allocation is typically directly assigned and the principal strategic problem is reporting. Hidden-action contracting allows behavior to remain privately chosen, but generally abstracts from the full conjunction of evolving private information, 4one-sided verification, technological authorization, and endogenous information use. Dynamic information design makes information itself a policy instrument, but typically does not embed that instrument in a dynamic agency problem in which the agent both privately reports evolving information and subsequently chooses a potentially hidden action. The static AI-agent benchmark allows rich private characteristics together with post-report action, but does not address how those characteristics evolve or how current reports, actions, and monitoring information reshape future mechanism choices. The missing object is therefore not simply a model containing these ingredients simultaneously, but a dynamic agency framework in which reporting, post-report action, and information use jointly shape the continuation mechanism and are themselves linked through continuation incentives. AI-agent environments naturally generate precisely this interaction. Private characteristics can evolve; reporting can be constrained by verification; additional private information can arrive after communication; the agent can retain hidden post-report action discretion; monitoring can generate evidence about states, types, or behavior; and the designer can use authorization, rewards, information disclosure, and future opportunities as dynamic instruments. Existing approaches therefore do not provide a common framework for analyzing these margins when they are jointly determined or for establishing how their interaction modifies the familiar conclusions of dynamic screening, hidden-action contracting, and dynamic information design. The paper contributes at two levels. First, at the framework level, we develop a general framework for dynamic mechanism and information design with AI agents that integrates evolving hidden information and hidden action with partial verification, technological authorization, and multi-agent interaction, while encompassing optimal contracting as a central design problem. At each date, the agent has a private type comprising its behavioral objective, execution capability, subjective belief, and information capability, and this private type may evolve over time. After communicating with the mechanism, the agent may receive additional private information and then privately choose its realized action. The action is distinct from the private-type profile and need not be publicly observed, although it may affect public outcomes, monitoring evidence, physical transitions, and future private information. A primitive monitoring technology generates evidence, while the mechanism chooses how that evidence is used in the public continuation state. The designer commits to a history-dependent mechanism governing recommendations, authorization, rewards, information use, and continuation decisions. The framework therefore nests conventional dynamic screening, hidden-action contracting, and conditional dynamic information design, while allowing reporting, action, and information-use incentives to interact through a common continuation mechanism. Although motivated by AI-agent environments, the framework is not specific to artificial agents and applies more broadly to dynamic agency settings with similar economic features. Two informational objects play distinct roles. We use verification for restrictions on which reports or claims can be substantiated and monitoring for technologies that generate evidence about latent states, private characteristics, or behavior. An AI evaluation may serve either role in practice, but economically the distinction matters: verification restricts the feasible report domain, whereas monitoring determines what evidence is available for subsequent information and control decisions. The primitive monitoring technology is fixed in the baseline model, but the mechanism chooses how the resulting evidence is processed and incorporated into the public continuation state. When behavior is hidden, the monitoring technology may depend on the latent action even though the information-use policy cannot condition directly on that action unless it is publicly observed. This distinction places the model between conventional dynamic information design and a fully 5endogenous informational system. Information production is taken as primitive, but information use is endogenous and dynamically linked to incentives, behavior, beliefs, and future control. The model therefore endogenizes the informational adjustment generated by the mechanism while stopping short of endogenizing information production itself. Endogenous information acquisition, experimentation, and the joint evolution of information production and feasibility lead to the broader system-level problem studied in Tian (2026). Second, at the theoretical level , the framework yields four main sets of results on dynamic implementability, optimal contracting, information design, and multi-agent incentives. These results can be summarized as follows. First, we establish the revelation and implementability foundations of the framework. A dynamic revelation principle shows that, under partial verification, indirect mechanisms can be replaced by direct mechanisms in which truthful reporting and post-report obedience are jointly incentive compatible. Because the agent receives additional private information and chooses its own action after reporting, a deviation may combine a false report with subsequent private action reoptimization. Dynamic incentive compatibility therefore has a nested structure: each feasible report must be evaluated together with the best post-report action available to the true type, while the truthful-report comparison must itself sustain the recommended action. We give an equivalent recursive characterization that separates honesty, obedience, and promise consistency while linking all three through continuation values. When the action is directly assigned, the inner action problem disappears and the recursion reduces to the standard dynamic reporting problem. Second, we characterize optimal dynamic mechanism design when hidden information, hidden action, and one-sided verification coexist. We focus on a regular scalar quasilinear environment for the hidden-information dimension while retaining post-report private action choice. Dynamic envelope and integral-monotonicity methods remain the analytical foundation, but the additional margins modify their implications in distinct ways. One-sided verification can place truthful reporting at the boundary of the feasible report set, so dynamic incentive compatibility need not imply the usual two-sided envelope equality. We capture this departure through a nonnegative verification wedge , which vanishes and restores the standard envelope implication when verification is locally nonbinding. Hidden action operates differently: because the true type can reoptimize its action after every feasible report, it changes the value associated with each report and restricts the set of mechanisms capable of implementing the desired truthful-path behavior. Optimal design consequently reflects three distinct margins: the familiar dynamic information rent , an additional verification rent , and the implementation cost of hidden action . The first two enter the rent representation, whereas the third generally operates through the implementable set rather than as another additive rent. This yields a verification-adjusted dynamic virtual-surplus representation, a virtual-surplus upper bound, and a relaxed virtual-surplus candidate. The candidate solves the full problem when it satisfies the nested report–action incentive condition together with the conditions under which the rent representation is tight. The resulting benchmark structure is branched rather than linear. The full 4 B environment combines hidden information, one-sided verification, and hidden action. The two second-best branches isolate the classical frictions: 2BHI is conventional dynamic hidden information with directly assignable action, whereas 2 BHA is the complete-information hidden-action problem. Adding one-sided verification to the former gives 3 BHI+V , while adding hidden action to dynamic screening under unrestricted reporting gives 3 BHI+HA . First best requires both private-information and action-implementation frictions to be absent. Thus eliminating private information does not in general 6restore first best when hidden action remains, just as eliminating hidden action does not restore first best when private information remains. Value comparisons follow from genuine feasible-set inclusion along a given branch, but there is no general ranking between the two second-best branches, and comparisons across different verification technologies require the corresponding report domains to be taken into account. The virtual-surplus representation and hidden-action implementation condition also yield a closed-form distortion decomposition. Under type-linear payoffs, hidden information is summarized by a dynamic virtual type that adjusts the current private type for the information rent propagated through the impulse response. Hidden action does not create a second virtual-type term; instead, it restricts the relaxed control to what can be supported by the available action incentives. In a linear-quadratic benchmark, we characterize all six benchmark controls and separate the distortion due to dynamic hidden information from the additional obedience distortion generated by bounded marginal action incentives. Under an AR(1) type process, the informational component decays geometrically with the horizon, while the hidden-action component depends on whether the required action incentive binds. The highest initial type therefore has no information-rent distortion under the usual regularity condition but may still face a hidden-action distortion, and the total 4 B distortion need not decline monotonically over calendar time. When control represents autonomy, tool access, compute, or another delegated resource, these results provide a mechanism-design rationale for staged control without requiring the realized control path itself to be monotone. Third, we develop the dynamic information-design component of the framework. We distinguish 

monitoring capacity , the informativeness of the primitive monitoring technology, from the 

information-use policy that determines how the resulting evidence is processed, disclosed, and incorporated into the continuation mechanism. The primitive experiment determines the informational frontier available to the designer, while the information-use policy selects a public experiment from that frontier. Because public information changes future authorization, rewards, deployment, and other mechanism choices, information design is intrinsically dynamic even when the primitive monitoring technology itself is fixed. The interaction with mechanism design becomes sharper when behavior is hidden. The raw monitoring experiment may depend on the privately chosen action, so different actions generate different distributions of evidence. Although the information-use policy cannot condition directly on the latent action, it determines how strongly those behavioral differences are transmitted into the public continuation state. Information use can therefore change both what the designer learns and the continuation incentives supporting current behavior. Information design and hidden-action implementation are thus jointly determined through the same continuation mechanism. We characterize the feasible public experiments as the Blackwell garblings of the primitive monitoring technology. Under free informational disposal, more informative monitoring capacity weakly expands the designer’s opportunity set because every policy available under a less informative experiment can be replicated. The replication argument holds conditional on the latent state, private type, and hidden action and therefore preserves both truthful behavior and feasible report–action deviations. Greater monitoring capacity, however, does not imply that greater public disclosure is optimal. Information revealed today changes future control and can thereby alter current reporting and action incentives, so full disclosure may be dominated by selective disclosure even when additional information has positive direct decision value. We characterize the boundary between information and incentive design: separation obtains precisely when the relevant continuation-value differences between truthful-and-obedient behavior and feasible report–action deviations are invariant to the 7information-use policy. Otherwise, information use changes the incentive constraints themselves and becomes an intertemporal incentive instrument. Fourth, we extend the framework to multiple AI agents. The dynamic revelation principle and recursive incentive characterization extend agent by agent while allowing correlated private information, higher-order beliefs, latent actions, and a common continuation mechanism. Under an independence benchmark, the dynamic rent and virtual-surplus analysis extends agent by agent, as does the branched hidden-information–hidden-action structure. Hidden action remains embedded in each agent’s nested obedience condition, while joint feasibility adds a distinct allocation margin. With a shared resource, for example, optimal allocation depends on marginal dynamic virtual values rather than current private values alone. Correlation creates another incentive instrument. An agent’s private information may predict the private information of other agents, allowing peer information to discipline false reporting. With finite report support and sufficiently distinct predictive distributions, sufficiently strong proper peer scoring can eliminate profitable feasible false reports. Strict affiliation of ordered finite-support types provides a primitive sufficient condition for this predictive separation. This logic has a sharp limitation in smooth continuous environments: a nearby false report may generate a first-order gain while a smooth quadratic peer score imposes only a second-order loss. Peer information therefore need not eliminate the underlying reporting and verification friction, nor does it by itself resolve the hidden-action obedience problem. Multi-agent incentives also operate through current observable consequences and continuation opportunities. A privately chosen action may affect the distribution of public outcomes or other contractible signals and thereby the agent’s expected current reward, while also changing future authorization, deployment, task assignments, or other opportunities. The marginal incentive for obedience therefore contains both an induced current-reward component and a continuation-value component. When these consequences depend jointly on the actions of several agents, the agentwise obedience conditions become strategically coupled even when primitive current payoffs are separable. Direct reward dependence on the full action profile is only the observable-action benchmark; the general result requires only that actions affect publicly contractible outcomes, monitoring evidence, physical transitions, or continuation states. Beyond these principal benchmarks, the revelation and verification structure builds on work on communication, partial verification, and evidence (Myerson, 1982; Forges, 1986; Green and Laffont, 1986; Ben-Porath, Dekel, and Lipman, 2019), while the dynamic incentive analysis relates to broader work on dynamic screening and multistage revelation (Battaglini, 2005; Bergemann and V¨ alim¨ aki, 2010; Garrett and Pavan, 2015; Sugaya and Wolitzky, 2021). The present framework combines evolving private information with verification-restricted communication and post-report action discretion, so deviations may involve both communication and private action reoptimization and may alter the continuation mechanism. On the information-design side, the distinctive feature relative to the benchmark literatures discussed above is that information use is embedded in this same dynamic agency problem: monitoring evidence enters the continuation mechanism, may itself be generated by hidden behavior, and therefore affects both future decisions and current report–action incentives. The multi-agent analysis is related to work on dynamic mechanisms with network interactions (Meng and Tian, 2021; Meng et al., 2022) and peer prediction and weak supervision (Qiu, Carroll, and Allen, 2026). The distinction between execution capability and authorization also connects the analysis to work on AI oversight, interpretability, adaptive deployment, and technological 8architectures for controlling tool-using agents (Wen et al., 2024; Herrmann and Levinstein, 2026; World Economic Forum, 2026; Microsoft Security, 2026; Saha, 2026; Surapani et al., 2026). These literatures isolate different informational, technological, or behavioral components of the control problem; the present paper studies how those components interact within a common dynamic mechanism and information-design environment. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the single-agent dynamic environment, hidden action, and partial verification. Section 3 develops indirect and direct mechanisms, establishes the dynamic revelation principle, and provides the recursive characterization of joint reporting and action incentives. Section 4 studies optimal dynamic mechanism design with hidden information and hidden action, derives the verification-adjusted dynamic virtual-surplus representation, and characterizes the branched distortion hierarchy. Section 5 develops the dynamic monitoring and information-design problem and studies feasible public experiments, selective disclosure, and the joint design of information and incentives. Section 6 extends the framework to multiple agents and studies dynamic virtual surplus, hidden-action implementation, peer information, coupled incentives, joint feasibility, and continuation externalities. Section 7 identifies the inherited, modified, and additional features of the framework relative to standard dynamic contracting. Section 8 develops applications to staged autonomy, strategic evaluation, and competition through continuation opportunities. Section 9 concludes. 

# 2 A Dynamic Model with a Single AI Agent 

This section specifies the dynamic single-agent environment that underlies the mechanism-design analysis. The representation of the agent’s private characteristics is related to the static AI-agent framework of Bergemann, Koh, and Morris (2026), while the evolution of private information draws on the broader tradition of dynamic mechanism design, particularly Pavan, Segal, and Toikka (2014). The present environment combines these elements by allowing the payoff-relevant state and private type to evolve over time and by allowing the agent’s post-report action to remain privately chosen, potentially unobserved, and dynamically consequential. We first specify the primitives of the environment, then the dynamic evolution and within-period timing, and finally the partial-verification structure.

Remark 1 (Nested agency environments) . The product representation of τt, together with the distinction between the agent’s execution capability, endogenous action, and publicly observed outcome, nests a number of familiar agency environments. If only the payoff component ut varies privately and the utility-relevant action is directly assigned, the model reduces to conventional adverse selection or screening. If the execution capability At is common knowledge but the agent chooses at ∈ At and the public outcome ot does not fully reveal that action, the model contains the standard hidden-action or moral-hazard problem. If At itself is private, the designer additionally faces private information about the agent’s execution capability. When the action is publicly observed, or equivalently when the public outcome reveals it, the same formulation yields post-report action discretion with observable behavior rather than hidden action. If only bt varies privately, private information concerns the agent’s subjective beliefs about the payoff-relevant state, whereas if only πt varies privately, agents differ in the information technology through which they learn about that state. These dimensions may also vary jointly. The general formulation therefore accommodates adverse selection, hidden action, private execution capability, heterogeneous beliefs, private information capabilities, and post-report action discretion within a common dynamic agency framework. ## 2.3 Partial verification 

The final ingredient of the baseline environment is partial verification of the agent’s private char-acteristics. The designer may obtain verifiable evidence about the agent’s realized private type. Such evidence can be asymmetric: an agent may conceal an existing capability, information source, or other verifiable component of its type without being able to counterfeit an attribute it does not possess. We represent this asymmetry through partial verification . Throughout, one-sided 

13 verification refers to the directional feature of this structure in which some verifiable characteristics can be concealed or understated without being symmetrically counterfeited or overstated. The general formulation below is represented by a verification preorder; the scalar withholding structure introduced in Section 4 is a particular one-sided specialization. This formulation builds on Green and Laffont (1986), one-sided endowment-reporting mechanisms (Tian, 1989), withholding mechanisms (Tian, 1993), the evidence literature (Ben-Porath, Dekel, and Lipman, 2019), and the application to AI agents in Bergemann, Koh, and Morris (2026). 

Definition 1 (Verification preorder) . For each date t, a verification preorder ⪰t is a reflexive and transitive relation on Tt. For any τt, bτt ∈ T t, we write τt ⪰t bτt when type τt can substantiate every claim or pass every verification test available to type bτt. The corresponding claim set is 

Ct(τt) = {bτt ∈ T t : τt ⪰t bτt} .

The verification preorder therefore determines which private types can imitate which others. A more capable agent may, for example, conceal capability through strategic underperformance, whereas an agent lacking that capability cannot necessarily counterfeit it. Thus Ct(τt) collects the verification-feasible claims, or reportable counterparts, that type τt can support. Reflexivity ensures that a type can always report itself, while transitivity captures the idea that evidence may be withheld but not fabricated. We impose capability consistency : whenever τt ⪰t bτt, the reported execution capability must satisfy bAt ⊆ At. When information capability is also verifiable, the preorder may additionally require πt ⪰B bπt, where ⪰B denotes the Blackwell order. More generally, ⪰t may restrict whichever components of the private type can be concealed but not counterfeited; behavioral objectives and subjective beliefs need not themselves be verifiable.

## 4.2 Optimal design and verification-adjusted dynamic virtual surplus 

We now turn from the agent’s incentive problem to the designer’s optimization problem. Participation is imposed at this stage: after observing η1, the agent may reject the mechanism and obtain its normalized outside option of zero. Monitoring generates evidence that can be used to condition future mechanism choices. In AI applications, monitoring may include evaluations, audits, tests, benchmark measurements, and other procedures that generate evidence about otherwise imperfectly observed capabilities, behavior, or payoff-relevant states. We use monitoring as the formal term for this information technology. Monitoring is distinct from verification. Verification restricts which claims or reports the agent can substantiate, whereas monitoring generates evidence that may enter the public continuation state and influence future authorization, rewards, deployment, and other mechanism choices. Information has two conceptually distinct margins: its production and its use . The baseline environment takes the technologies that generate information as primitive. The agent’s information capability πt determines the private signal it receives before choosing its action, while the primitive monitoring technology determines the raw evidence available to the mechanism after current behavior. The realized action at need not be publicly observed, but it may affect the distribution of monitoring evidence. In this section, these information-production technologies remain fixed. What becomes endogenous is information use: the mechanism chooses how generated monitoring evidence is processed, disclosed, and incorporated into future beliefs, permissions, rewards, and other continuation decisions. Endogenizing information production itself would introduce learning and experimentation and is reserved for the broader system-level analysis, where information production, information use, posterior evolution, incentives, and future feasibility are jointly determined (Tian, 2026). The analysis combines two established strands of theory. On the dynamic mechanism-design side, continuation values summarize future incentives, while current choices affect those values through the evolution of the state and private information, as in Pavan, Segal, and Toikka (2014) and Pavan (2026). On the information-design side, Bayesian persuasion studies the choice of information subject to Bayes plausibility and receiver optimality (Kamenica and Gentzkow, 2011), while Bergemann and Morris (2019) formulate information design through recommendation and obedience. Doval and Ely (2020) provide a sequential information-design benchmark in which information and extensive-form structure interact over time. Bergemann, Heumann, and Morris (2026a) integrate information design and mechanism design within a common static screening framework, while Bergemann, Koh, and Morris (2026) emphasize that mechanisms for AI agents must support obedience jointly with truthful revelation of privately known characteristics. Their evaluation structure concerns verification of type claims; the monitoring technology introduced here is a distinct object that generates post-action evidence for use in the continuation mechanism. The present environment links dynamic mechanism design and information design through the continuation mechanism. Information use changes not only what becomes publicly known, but also the public state on which future authorization, rewards, deployment, and other mechanism choices may depend. A public monitoring message can therefore change the continuation payoff from a current report or post-report action. When at is hidden, the primitive monitoring technology may depend on that action even though the subsequent information-use policy cannot condition on it directly. Information design and incentive design are consequently connected through continuation values. We first characterize which public information can be generated from a fixed monitoring technology, then incorporate information use into the recursive mechanism problem, and finally study monitoring capacity, selective disclosure, and the conditions under which information use can be separated from current incentive design. 44 5.1 Monitoring Capacity and Feasible Information Use

# 7 AI-Relevant Features Beyond Standard Dynamic Contracting 

The preceding sections combine established dynamic mechanism-design and contracting foundations with a set of information, incentive, and control margins that are particularly natural for AI agents. The comparison is useful in three layers. Some analytical structure is inherited from standard dynamic screening and dynamic contracting; some familiar conclusions are modified 

by partial verification and post-report action discretion; and additional design margins arise from technologically enforceable authorization, dynamic monitoring and information use, and interaction among multiple agents. These distinctions do not define a separate theory for artificial agents. The individual ingredients may also arise with human agents, firms, experts, or organizations. Their joint presence is especially natural for AI systems: private information may concern objectives, execution capability, beliefs, or information capability; communication need not determine subsequent behavior; actions may remain privately chosen after communication; and access to tools, communication channels, external systems, computational resources, or deployment can itself be controlled by the mechanism. The general dynamic structure is inherited from standard mechanism design and contract theory. Private information may evolve over time, privately chosen actions may affect future states, and continuation values summarize the incentives generated by future mechanism choices. A sufficient public state can therefore represent the continuation problem recursively, and the revelation principle reduces the analysis to direct mechanisms subject to the relevant reporting and action-incentive constraints. The scalar environment of Section 4 makes the connection to conventional dynamic screening especially transparent. When reporting is unrestricted and the payoff-relevant action is directly assigned, the post-report action problem and the verification boundary disappear. Dynamic envelope methods then relate continuation utility to the propagation of private information through impulse responses, yielding the familiar information-rent and virtual-surplus representation of Pavan, Segal, and Toikka (2014). This is the 2 BHI branch of the benchmark map. The hidden-action branch is equally familiar in economic logic. When the relevant private type is common knowledge but the agent privately chooses an action that cannot be contracted on directly, the remaining problem is one of moral hazard: observable outcomes, rewards, authorization, and continuation promises must provide the incentives needed to implement desired behavior. This is the 2 BHA branch. The broader contracting literature has long studied environments combining adverse selection and moral hazard, including Laffont and Tirole (1986), Picard (1987), Guesnerie, Picard, and Rey (1989), and Meng and Tian (2013); dynamic environments combining the two frictions include Es˝ o and Szentes (2017). The present framework therefore does not replace either established strand. Instead, it places dynamic hidden information and hidden action inside the same recursive mechanism and adds one-sided verification and AI-relevant control instruments. The two second-best branches are conceptually distinct: eliminating hidden action leaves the 2 BHI dynamic-screening problem, whereas eliminating private information leaves the 2 BHA moral-hazard problem. First best requires both frictions to disappear. Adding one-sided verification to the former gives 3 BHI+V , while adding hidden action to dynamic screening gives 3 BHI+HA ; the full 4 B environment contains hidden information, one-sided verification, and hidden-action implementation. The recursive foundation remains common across these cases. Verification restricts the reports available to a true type; authorization restricts the actions the mechanism permits; and post-report action discretion requires the mechanism to account for what the true type will actually do after any feasible report. These features change the relevant implementation and rent conditions without changing the underlying dynamic-programming logic. 

## 7.2 Modified and Additional AI-Control Margins 

The first distinction is between execution capability and authorization . Execution capability describes what the agent can actually do, whereas authorization describes what the mechanism permits it to do. The two need not coincide. This distinction matters dynamically because authorization can depend on past reports, realized mechanism choices, public outcomes, monitoring information, and other publicly observed variables, and therefore becomes part of the continuation mechanism that shapes current incentives. Partial verification modifies the reporting margin. Under one-sided verification, a type may be able to conceal or understate a verifiable attribute without being able to counterfeit a higher 67 one. Truthful reporting can therefore lie at the boundary of the feasible report set rather than at an interior point of an unrestricted reporting problem. Section 4 shows that dynamic incentive compatibility then need not imply the ordinary two-sided envelope equality. The verification wedge measures the resulting continuation-rent margin, while local nonbinding verification restores the standard envelope implication. Post-report action discretion modifies a different part of the incentive problem. Communication does not mechanically determine behavior. After a report, the true type may observe additional private information and choose among the actions that are both executable and authorized. When that action is not publicly observed, this is a dynamic moral-hazard problem embedded inside the reporting mechanism. A false report must therefore be evaluated after allowing the true type to reoptimize its current action. Dynamic incentive compatibility consequently requires honesty and obedience jointly: feasible false reports are report–action deviations, while the truthful-report comparison imposes sequential obedience. These modifications clarify the three economic margins identified in Section 4. The infor-mational transaction cost is inherited from dynamic screening and is governed by the initial rent weight and the impulse responses through which private information remains relevant over time. The verification transaction cost is the additional continuation rent generated when one-sided reportability prevents the ordinary envelope representation from being attained. The 

action-implementation cost , or obedience cost, is different: a latent post-report action restricts the set of mechanisms capable of sustaining the desired behavior. It therefore appears as a shadow cost of implementability rather than as a separate additive rent term. These margins operate through different mathematical channels even though they are jointly determined by the same mechanism. This decomposition also explains why the dynamic virtual type summarizes only the hidden-information distortion. The inverse hazard rate and impulse response determine the propagated information-rent adjustment. Hidden action does not generate another virtual-type term; it restricts the implementable set through the nested obedience condition. One-sided verification operates through yet another channel, the verification wedge. The dynamic virtual type, verification wedge, and hidden-action implementation condition therefore provide complementary rather than competing descriptions of the full 4 B problem. Dynamic monitoring introduces another design margin. Section 5 distinguishes the primitive 

monitoring technology from the mechanism’s information-use policy . The informativeness of the primitive experiment determines monitoring capacity, while the information-use policy selects a public experiment from the Blackwell-feasible set. A latent action may affect the distribution of raw monitoring evidence even though the information-use policy cannot condition directly on that action. More informative monitoring capacity weakly expands the designer’s opportunity set when information can be freely discarded, but more informative public use of that capacity need not be optimal. The reason is dynamic. Public monitoring information changes the continuation state and may therefore affect future authorization, rewards, deployment, and other mechanism choices. It can consequently change the continuation payoff associated with current truthful behavior and current report–action deviations. Information use can be optimized separately from current incentive design only when the relevant truthful-versus-deviation continuation-value differences are invariant to the information-use policy. Otherwise, information use is itself an incentive instrument. With hidden action, this channel is especially important because information use may strengthen or weaken the 68 continuation incentive generated by evidence about privately chosen behavior without making that behavior directly observable. The baseline analysis keeps information production fixed. Thus the private information capability and primitive monitoring technology are taken as given, while the use of generated monitoring evidence is endogenous. Endogenizing information production would introduce the additional interaction among learning, incentives, posterior evolution, and future feasibility studied in Tian (2026). 

## 7.3 Multi-Agent Interactions and Synthesis 

The multi-agent extension preserves the preceding incentive structure but adds cross-agent informa-tion, feasibility, and continuation effects. Under the independence benchmark of Section 6.3, the single-agent rent accounting extends agent by agent: dynamic information rents and verification wedges enter the multi-agent virtual-surplus representation separately for each agent, while hidden-action implementation remains embedded in each agent’s nested obedience condition. Joint feasibility nevertheless links allocations across agents. With a shared capacity constraint, for example, scarce control is allocated according to marginal dynamic virtual values rather than current private values alone. Correlated private information creates a different channel. One agent’s type may predict the reports of the others, so peer information can discipline false reporting. Section 6.4 shows that the relevant peer-score intensity is determined by the report–action gain from a deviation relative to the informational divergence generated by the peer score. Partial verification matters because only feasible false reports need to be deterred, while dynamics matter because the gain from a current deviation includes its effect on continuation value. The distinction between finite and continuous report spaces is important. With a finite report set and sufficiently separated predictive distributions, a finite peer-score scale may discipline reporting. With continuous types, however, a smooth quadratic score generates only a second-order loss from a nearby false report. If the corresponding deviation gain is first order, no finite score scale can eliminate that local deviation. Peer information therefore does not generally remove the verification and reporting frictions identified in the single-agent analysis, nor does it by itself resolve the hidden-action obedience problem. Coupled incentives operate on the action margin. An agent’s privately chosen action may change the distribution of public outcomes or other observable signals on which its expected current reward depends, and may also change its future authorization and continuation value. The marginal incentive for obedience can therefore have both an induced current-reward component and a continuation-value component. The continuation mechanism may strengthen or weaken the current expected-reward incentive needed to sustain a target action, and the resulting agentwise obedience conditions may be strategically coupled even when current primitive payoffs are separable. Direct dependence of rewards on the action profile is only the observable-action special case; the general formulation requires only that actions affect the distribution of publicly contractible outcomes or signals. The comparison can therefore be summarized in the three layers introduced at the start of the section. Inherited elements include dynamic revelation, continuation values, recursive incentives, impulse responses, envelope methods, information rents, virtual surplus, moral-hazard obedience, and distortion dynamics. Modified results arise because one-sided verification changes the reporting 69 boundary and envelope logic, while post-report private information and hidden action require reports and subsequent actions to be evaluated jointly. Additional design margins arise from the distinction between execution capability and authorization, dynamic monitoring and information use, joint feasibility, peer information, coupled incentives, and cross-agent continuation effects. The contribution is therefore not that hidden information, hidden action, or the other individual ingredients are unique to artificial agents. It is that these ingredients can be analyzed jointly in a dynamic mechanism in which private information evolves, privately informed agents retain post-report action discretion, realized actions may remain latent, and the designer controls technological authorization, information use, and continuation opportunities. This structure identifies when standard dynamic-screening or moral-hazard results remain sufficient, when reporting and action implementation must be treated jointly, and when verification, information, and control instruments create additional economically consequential margins. 

# 8 Applications 

We conclude with three tractable applications of the design margins developed in Sections 4– 6. The first applies the dynamic virtual-surplus and implementation analysis of Section 4 to staged control under persistent private information and makes the branched benchmark structure explicit. The second interprets the monitoring technology of Section 5 as an AI evaluation and shows how information use can change hidden behavior being evaluated. The third applies the coupled-continuation channel of Section 6 to competition for a future deployment opportunity. The examples are deliberately stylized. Their purpose is not to introduce additional general machinery, but to map the preceding analytical objects into economically interpretable AI-control problems. They also illustrate different observability structures. Actions may remain latent and affect rewards or continuation only through observable consequences, may be observed by a monitoring technology without otherwise becoming public, or may be publicly observed as a useful benchmark. In each case, current private information or behavior affects future control, and the value of that continuation regime feeds back into current incentives.

## 8.4 Discussion 

The three applications isolate the principal margins developed in Sections 4–6. In the staged-autonomy application, the complete benchmark map is visible in closed form: persistent private information generates the dynamic virtual-type distortion, bounded action incentives generate the hidden-action implementation distortion, and one-sided verification is nonbinding. The 2 BHI and 77 2BHA branches isolate the two primitive frictions, while 3 BHI+HA and 4 B combine them. In the strategic-evaluation application, performance is hidden from the public mechanism but affects the monitoring signal; information use changes future deployment and thereby feeds back into the behavior that generates the evidence. In the multi-agent application, observable current actions affect a scarce future authorization opportunity, creating cross-agent incentives even though current primitive payoffs and rewards are separable. The applications therefore deliberately span different action-observability regimes. Staged autonomy uses the reduced-form expected reward induced by a post-report action and hence does not require the action itself to be contractible. Strategic evaluation makes the distinction sharper: performance is observed by the primitive monitor but is not otherwise public, so only the monitoring evidence can enter the continuation mechanism. Competition for future deployment uses publicly observed actions to isolate the continuation externality. These cases are all nested by the general framework rather than being alternative assumptions imposed throughout the paper. The common object is the continuation mechanism. Continuation values transmit the effect of private information across time, make monitoring information an incentive instrument, and couple agents through future access and opportunities. Hidden action adds a further channel because privately chosen behavior may change observable outcomes, monitoring evidence, physical transitions, and therefore the continuation state without itself becoming a public contracting variable. These channels are analytically distinct but need not operate separately in richer environments: monitoring may determine future authorization, authorization may be allocated jointly across agents, and private types may affect both current behavior and continuation control. The applications therefore provide a decomposition of the main forces in the general framework rather than three unrelated examples. Their common message is that a dynamic AI mechanism cannot generally be reduced to a sequence of independently optimized static mechanisms. Current private information and privately chosen behavior shape the future control regime, while the value of that regime feeds back into reporting and action incentives today. 

# 9 Conclusion 

This paper develops a general framework for dynamic mechanism and information design with AI agents, encompassing optimal contracting and integrating evolving hidden information and hidden action with partial verification, technological authorization, and multi-agent interaction. The common object linking these dimensions is the continuation mechanism: current reports, privately chosen actions, and information use shape public beliefs, future states, and future control, while the value of those continuation consequences feeds back into current reporting and action incentives. Conventional dynamic screening, hidden-action contracting, and conditional dynamic information design therefore arise as special cases of the framework when the other margins are held fixed. The first set of results concerns implementability. A dynamic revelation principle continues to reduce the analysis to direct mechanisms under partial verification, but incentive compatibility must jointly support truthful reporting and post-report obedience. Because the agent can receive additional private information and reoptimize its action after any feasible report, the relevant deviation is a report–action deviation rather than a report evaluated at a mechanically assigned action. The recursive characterization therefore links honesty and obedience through continuation values while preserving the standard dynamic-programming structure. Optimal design contains three distinct margins. Dynamic hidden information generates the 78 familiar information rent; one-sided verification can create an additional continuation-rent wedge; and hidden action restricts which desired allocations can actually be implemented. The first two enter the rent representation, whereas the third generally operates through the implementable set rather than as another additive rent. This yields a verification-adjusted dynamic virtual-surplus representation and a branched benchmark structure. Conventional dynamic screening is the 2 BHI 

branch, complete-information hidden-action contracting is the 2 BHA branch, and the full 4 B problem combines hidden information, one-sided verification, and hidden-action implementation. First best requires both the private-information and action-implementation frictions to disappear. The same decomposition clarifies dynamic distortions. Under type-linear payoffs, a dynamic virtual type summarizes the propagated hidden-information distortion, while hidden action enters through the obedience and implementability conditions. In the linear-quadratic benchmark, the total distortion separates into a dynamic information-rent component and an additional hidden-action component, with verification nonbinding in that benchmark. Persistence therefore provides a mechanism-design rationale for staged control, but the total distortion need not decline monotonically over time when action incentives or other implementation constraints also bind. A separate contribution is the dynamic information-design problem. Conditional on a fixed primitive monitoring technology, the mechanism chooses how the resulting evidence is transformed into public information and incorporated into the continuation state. This provides a conditional dynamic information-design benchmark inside the broader mechanism-design environment. Holding reporting and action incentives fixed recovers the familiar logic of dynamic information design; allowing them to respond endogenously makes information use part of the incentive problem itself. The distinction between monitoring capacity and information use is central. More informative monitoring capacity weakly expands the designer’s opportunity set when information can be freely discarded, because less informative experiments can be reproduced by garbling. Greater disclosure, however, need not be optimal. Information released today changes future authorization, rewards, deployment, and other continuation decisions and may therefore alter current reporting or hidden-action incentives. Information and incentive design are separable precisely when the relevant continuation-value differences between truthful-and-obedient behavior and feasible deviations are invariant to the information-use policy. Otherwise, information use is itself a dynamic incentive instrument. When monitoring depends on latent behavior, the policy can influence the incentive to generate the evidence being disclosed without ever conditioning directly on the hidden action. The multi-agent extension preserves this structure while adding new channels of interaction. Under independence, the rent and virtual-surplus analysis extends agent by agent, hidden-action implementation remains embedded in each agent’s obedience condition, and joint feasibility links allocations across agents. Correlated private information can make peer reports informative about an agent’s type and thereby discipline false reporting, although smooth peer scoring cannot generally eliminate first-order local deviations in continuous environments. Current actions can also affect expected rewards and future authorization through public outcomes, monitoring evidence, and continuation states, creating strategically coupled incentives even when primitive current payoffs are separable. The applications make these mechanisms concrete. Staged autonomy separates the dynamic hidden-information and hidden-action distortions within the complete benchmark map. Strategic evaluation shows how hidden performance can affect monitoring evidence and how the use of that evidence can feed back into the behavior being evaluated. Competition for future deployment illustrates how continuation authorization can generate present incentives and cross-agent interaction 79 even without coupled current rewards. The applications differ in action observability, but all operate through the same feedback from current information and behavior to future control. The framework deliberately stops short of making information production itself endogenous. Primitive private-signal and monitoring technologies are taken as given, while their strategic use and the resulting continuation consequences are endogenous. Endogenizing information acquisition, experimentation, learning, posterior evolution, capability development, and dynamic feasibility would move from conditional dynamic information design to the broader problem of endogenous informational evolution studied in Tian (2026). Other natural extensions include limited commitment, richer participation and capability dynamics, robustness to misspecified beliefs and transition laws, and richer forms of multi-agent competition, coordination, and information sharing. The broader conclusion is therefore not that AI agents require a mechanism design theory separate from the existing economic tradition. Dynamic screening, hidden-action contracting, and dynamic information design remain the principal benchmarks. What changes is that AI-agent environments naturally bring these margins together with technologically enforceable authorization and history-dependent continuation control. The framework provides a common way to identify when the familiar benchmark results remain valid, when the different margins must be analyzed jointly, and when information and future control themselves become central instruments of dynamic mechanism design.
